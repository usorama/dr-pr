### The plan in five sentences

1. He becomes known as the cardiologist who **teaches** patients, the public and young doctors, in Telugu and English. Every piece carries its sources, its numbers and his registration number. He never sells himself.
2. Everything is built on **things he owns**. These are his name, his web domain, his e-mail list, his accounts and his papers. They also include his society memberships and his own clinic's patient system. Nothing important depends on Apollo.
3. He does not try to cover new topics. He uses four forms of teaching that nobody else uses:
   - the numbered **"After the stent"** course;
   - **"The first 60 minutes"**, taught hands-on;
   - the weekly **"Know your numbers"** Telugu card, with sources, his signature and an English written version;
   - **case discussions for doctors**, with a published method for checking AI.
4. He starts **one main programme at a time**. First comes "Patients for life" in his own practice. Second come case discussions for doctors, open to registered doctors only. Third comes a measured "Heart School" group. Each programme counts its results and publishes them, including disappointing results.
5. His **advantage with AI** is that he uses it openly and responsibly, not that he owns special tools. He gives patients Telugu explanations that AI drafts and he checks. He keeps an audited record of his own patients' results. He publishes a set of rules for his use of AI, and he teaches other doctors how to judge AI. He uses no AI copy of himself, no copy of his voice and no public chatbot.

## 3. The rules, the other channels, the audiences and the openings

### 3.1 The rules, in their own words

**Main point:** two sets of rules apply. They are the October 2026 Guidelines and the 2002 Regulations, which still bind him. Together they leave one wide area open: education, research, teaching and factual information. Owning a practice does not make the rules on promotion any weaker. It only lets him control the facts that are published about his practice [11b, 30].

**The legal status of the Guidelines (The rule says).** The document is a Public Notice of **Guidelines** (No. R-13014/01/2024-Ethics, 6 October 2026). It is to be "read with" the 2002 Regulations. Clause 4.2 says that where the two conflict and cannot be reconciled, "the applicable statutory provisions and operative regulations shall prevail". In plain words: if the Guidelines and the binding law disagree, the binding law wins. The Guidelines are not a regulation published in the official Gazette. The State Medical Council enforces them. Clause 10.1 lists penalties, rising step by step, that "may be contemplated" [30 A8]. In plain words: the council may consider these penalties.

## 4A. Who may say what

**Main point:** the Guidelines control what **he** says, arranges or takes up as his own. They also control his clinic and his hospital. They do not control what other people say on their own. These people are patients, young doctors, other doctors speaking for themselves, journalists and medical societies. Clause 4.1 says the Guidelines "apply to the professional conduct of the RMPs", and to hospitals. In plain words: the Guidelines apply to how doctors and hospitals behave. So he does not have to stay silent about patients and results. Instead, four things must be right:

- the right **speaker**;
- the right **place** (private, among doctors, or published);
- the right **order** (a journal or conference first, and a public mention after that);
- and he never asks for, writes, rewards, shares, quotes or reposts praise of himself to promote himself.

This section follows a standing instruction from his friend (research brief, Addendum 5). Research file 32 covers the same subject in full. It looks at 13 speakers and 15 acts, at rules in other countries, and at model wording. Section 4A.4 below records what file 32 adds and where it differs from the tables here. Where the two differ, file 32 gives the more detailed reading.

### 4A.1 The clauses that decide it (exact words, from file 30)

- **Explanation V to clause 3.2, first sentence:** "No RMP or hospital/medical institution shall solicit, purchase, procure, manipulate or cause to be published fake, paid or misleading patient reviews, ratings, testimonials, endorsements or recommendations for the promotion of medical services."
  In plain words: no doctor or hospital may obtain, buy, change or publish fake, paid or misleading reviews or praise to promote medical services.
- **Explanation V, second sentence:** "An RMP shall not request or share patient testimonials, recommendations, endorsements or reviews **for professional promotion on social media**."
  In plain words: a doctor must not ask for patients' praise or reviews, or share them, to promote himself on social media. The limit is in the words: the rule is about asking or sharing, for promotion, on social media.
- **Clause 3.6:** "Social Media" means "all forms of publications and sharing through internet-based websites, apps, etc. by whatsoever name called, which allows users to create, share and join online communities…"
  In plain words: social media means any website or app where users create, share and join online groups. Our reading: this is broad enough to include review websites.
- **Clause 4.4:** he is responsible for content he "authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted".
  In plain words: he is responsible for content that he approved, ordered, paid for, took up as his own or knowingly allowed.
- **Clause 5.1:** education is allowed "provided that such activities do not promote personal practice, do not solicit patients and are not monetized through promotional marketing". The clause names "academic discussions".
  In plain words: he may educate if it does not promote his practice, does not ask for patients and does not earn money through promotion. Academic discussions are one form of education.
- **Clause 6.1:** patient information or testimonials "shall not be used for advertising purposes except where … permitted by applicable law and the necessary consent and other safeguards". **Clause 6.2:** consent "shall not, by itself, make permissible any testimonial, endorsement, promotional patient story… success claim".
  In plain words: patient information and patient praise may not be used for advertising. The exception is where the law allows it and consent and other protections are in place. Consent alone does not make patient praise, promotional patient stories or success claims allowed.
- **Clause 8.1(ii):** a doctor shall not "solicit patients…, promote his/her professional services through self-promotion, engage any third party for marketing medical services, permit the use of their name, image, voice, testimonial or endorsement for promotion".
  In plain words: he must not seek patients, promote himself, or use another person to market his services. He must not let others use his name, image, voice or praise for promotion.
- **Clause 8.1(iii):** a doctor shall not "advertise either personal achievements or professional success rates or number of patients treated with illustrative assertions such as 'Guaranteed cures', 'Best doctors'… or any other unverifiable or exaggerated claims".
  In plain words: he must not advertise his achievements, success rates or patient numbers with claims such as "guaranteed cures" or "best doctors". He must not use any claim that nobody can check, or that overstates the truth.
- **Clause 8.1(v):** a doctor shall not publish cases for promotion or "promote personal success stories **unless published strictly for scientific or educational purposes with anonymized patient consent**".
  In plain words: he must not use cases or success stories for promotion. He may publish them only for science or education, with the patient's consent and without the patient's identity.
- **Clause 8.1(ix):** no "best", "No.1", "leading", "most trusted", "top"… "unless the claim is objectively verifiable…". **Proviso:** "Any ranking or award relied upon in advertising shall be capable of independent verification, and material conditions, methodology and relevant date, details of award giving organization… shall be disclosed".
  In plain words: he must not use words such as "best" or "top" unless the claim can be checked. If he uses a ranking or an award, someone independent must be able to check it. He must state the conditions, the method, the date and the body that gave the award.
- **Clause 8.1(xi):** "No RMP shall **use or permit** any celebrity, influencer, patient, employee or third party to promote professional services through prohibited testimonials, endorsements or recommendations… No RMP… shall use a third party as an **intermediary** to do indirectly what the RMP… is prohibited from doing directly."
  In plain words: he must not use or allow any famous person, influencer, patient, employee or other person to promote him with forbidden praise. He must not use another person to do for him what he may not do himself.
- **Clause 8.1(xii):** no obtaining or changing "fake followers, likes, reviews, ratings, testimonials, comments, views".
  In plain words: he must not obtain fake followers, likes, reviews, ratings, praise, comments or views.
- **Clause 8.2(i):** "Only research-based communication, wherein they have published interesting case studies in reputed medical journals shall be allowed." **8.2(ii):** formal announcements. **8.2(iii):** talks "in their own name and designation, without promoting their employer organization".
  In plain words: he may communicate about research when he has published the cases in good medical journals. He may make formal announcements. He may give talks in his own name and title if he does not promote his employer.
- **Clause 8.3(ii):** hospital communication shall not contain "promotional portrayal of an individual RMP".
  In plain words: a hospital must not show one doctor in a promotional way.

### 4A.2 What the clauses mean, in plain statements

1. **The Guidelines do not control what a patient says on his or her own, without being asked.** They bind doctors and hospitals (clause 4.1). (The scope is "The rule says". How it applies here is "Our reading".) The patient's words become his act in some cases. These are if he asked for them, wrote them, rewarded them, shared them, quoted them, reposted them or linked them. They also become his act if he knowingly let someone use them for promotion (Explanation V; clause 4.4; 8.1(xi)).
2. **He may ask patients for private feedback to improve care. The words of the rules do not cover this.** Explanation V covers reviews "for professional promotion on social media". Clause 6.1 covers use "for advertising purposes". (Our reading, with high confidence.) Four conditions apply:
   - The purpose is to improve care.
   - The feedback goes to a private place (a clinic form or a survey).
   - He uses it to learn inside the clinic. He may also use it in a published audit, but only as combined numbers that identify nobody.
   - He never publishes or quotes it as praise.
3. **The text forbids asking for a public review. It also forbids sharing, quoting or reposting a review to promote himself** (Explanation V, second sentence, read with clause 3.6). Is a neutral, routine request for a Google review "for professional promotion"? Strictly, that is **Not decided**. This plan treats it as forbidden, because it brings only risk and no gain.
4. **His own combined results go first to journals and conferences.** Combined results are percentages and counts that show the total number of patients and give no names or other identifying details. The rules allow this as "academic discussions" (clause 5.1). They allow it as "research-based communication… in reputed medical journals" (8.2(i)). It also fits the exception in 8.1(v) for work published "strictly for scientific or educational purposes with anonymized patient consent". (The rule says.) Once the work is published, he may mention it in public as published research. He does this neutrally, with a link and the total number of patients, and he does not compare himself with others. (Our reading.)
5. **Clause 8.1(iii) is aimed at *advertising*.** It covers success rates, achievements or patient numbers stated with claims that cannot be checked, or that overstate the truth. (The rule says.) An audit that is published and can be checked is a different act. It shows the total number of patients and is cited as research. Two acts are **Not decided**. One is telling the public results that are not published. The other is keeping figures on his website for how well his patients' risk factors are controlled. (This is question 14 for the lawyer, in section 3 of `questions-and-open-items.md`.)

### 4A.4 What research file 32 adds

**Main point:** file 32 agrees with the tables above and with his friend's view of who may speak. It makes four answers stricter, and it provides the working documents.

**Where the text clearly supports the difference between his acts and other people's speech [32 §1.15, §2.1]:**

- A patient, a family member, a young doctor, another doctor, a society or a journalist speaks without being asked. He is responsible only for content he "authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted" (clause 4.4). In plain words: content that he approved, ordered, paid for, took up as his own or knowingly allowed. He is also responsible for people he "use[d] or permit[ted]" (clause 8.1(xi)). In plain words: people he used or allowed to act for him.
- Requests for private feedback and structured surveys. No clause forbids them.
- Teaching, writing about research, and presenting or publishing combined results that identify nobody to other doctors and to journals.

**Where the text does not support it:**

- Resharing, quoting, embedding or showing praise or ratings on any channel he controls. This is forbidden for him and for anyone acting for him: his staff, his friend, a hospital or AI.
- Asking for public reviews, including a QR code at the clinic reception desk. The words of the rule do not decide this. A careful reading forbids it, and this plan treats it as forbidden.
- Telling the public his own results directly. This is Not decided. "Achievements" and "reputation" are inside the definition of advertising (clause 3.2). The 2002 Regulations say he shall not "boast of cases, operations, cures". In plain words: he must not boast about his cases, operations or cures. The way to do it is to publish first and mention the work after that.

**Four answers that file 32 makes stricter than the first version of the tables in §4A.3:**

|---|---|---|---|
| Awards and fellowships | He may list awards that can be checked, as facts. Yes | He may **accept** them. **Listing** them on his own pages is Ask a lawyer | He accepts them freely. He lists his registered qualifications and his society fellowships on the factual "About" page. He does not list other awards until the lawyer answers |

**How regulators in other countries draw the same line [32 §3].** Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, Alberta, the UK and the US all leave the patient's own words alone. They decide a doctor's responsibility with four tests:

- Does he **control** the platform?
- Did he **ask** for the words?
- Did he **choose** among them?
- Did he **use** them in advertising?

New Zealand's wording is closest to his friend's view. It says that a doctor must not encourage patient praise. It also says: "you are not responsible for any unsolicited testimonials or comments" on media "over which you do not have control". In plain words: a doctor is not responsible for praise or comments that he did not ask for, on media that he does not control. India's rule on requests uses narrower words than Singapore's rule ("any media"). But India's definition of social media is wide enough to reach a similar result. File 32 found no Indian court ruling on praise that a third person gives without being asked. (The research did not read the texts of the GMC, Ontario and Singapore at the source.)

**The three ways forward, with the working documents:**

1. **A private system for feedback and results, published as science.**
   - File 32 §4.4 gives a consent line for the registration form.
   - §4.5 gives a neutral feedback form in English and a working draft in Telugu. A clinician who speaks Telugu must check the draft, and five patients must test it. The form says "We do not ask patients for public reviews".
   - §4.6 gives the "You said, we did" notice for the waiting room (clause 8.2(iv), Our reading).
2. **He publishes first and mentions the work after that.** The steps go in this order [32 §5.1]:
   1. A consent line.
   2. Approval from an ethics committee, or a decision that approval is not needed.
   3. An independent check of the total number of patients.
   4. An abstract, then a paper.
   5. A submission to a registry, where one exists.
   6. A neutral public mention inside his teaching.

   §5.4 gives the wording. It uses counts with the total number of patients. It states how many patients were lost to follow-up. It says "single clinic, no comparison group". It makes no comparison with others.
3. **Other bodies publish, with a method.** Anything that would be praise if he said it is published by the partner, the society, the registry or the journal, in its own name. The host publishes the CPR results. The society publishes the feedback from young doctors. The journal publishes the patient results [32 §6].

**When a patient asks "where can I leave you a review?"** Until the lawyer replies, he uses the middle answer from file 32 [32 §4.7]: *"That is kind of you. I can't ask for reviews or suggest what anyone writes. What you say about your own experience, anywhere, is entirely your decision, and there is no need to. Please don't put your medical details online on my account. What helps me most is this private form."* Four rules apply in every version of the answer:

- He never raises the subject first.
- He never suggests what to write or what rating to give.
- He offers nothing in return.
- He never reposts what the patient writes afterwards.

**For any new case,** file 32 §7 has six questions to help him decide and twelve worked examples. File 32 §8 has the questions for the lawyer and for the Telangana council.

### 5.7 His advantage with AI

- **What it is.** The advantage lies in how he works and in responsibility that people can see. In order [18]:

### 6.3 The steps for approving and checking each item

Every item passes these steps in order. A "No" at any step stops the item.

1. **Topic.** The topic comes from the calendar. His friend proposes topics. He approves them each quarter.
2. **Draft.** The draft comes from his recorded words or his notes. AI is given the sources. Nobody ever asks AI to remember facts [22 §4.5].
3. **Fact list (each fact with its source).** Each claim is listed. A person opens the source and pastes in the figure. If a source will not open, the sentence is deleted.
4. **Compliance checklist.** This is the checklist in file 11 §9(i), with the items that file 11b adds. It covers the subject, the words, patients, money, asking for patients or reviews, the credential line and AI.
5. **Telugu check.** For any instruction, the Telugu checker also translates it back into English.
6. **A second doctor's check,** for the items that need one.
7. **His approval.** The log records the date, the platform, the sources, who approved and the time.
8. **Publishing.** The item carries the "Medically checked / next review" stamp and its sources.
9. **Comments.** His friend watches comments and replies only with prepared replies.

### 6.4 The quality standard

- **Order of sources** [22 §4.1]. From strongest to weakest:
  1. Guidelines (Indian guidelines first, where they exist).
  2. Systematic reviews.
  3. Named trials.
  4. Indian registries.
  5. Observational studies. For these he says "associated with", not "causes".
  6. His own opinion, labelled as opinion.

  Press releases, early papers not yet reviewed (preprints), other doctors' videos and AI output are not sources.
- **Numbers.** He gives the absolute size of an effect before the relative size. He names the group of people. He never says "up to".
- **How often he reviews items.** Every 12 months for pieces that stay true for a long time. Every 6 months for anything that names a type of medicine, a target or a device.
- **How he corrects errors** [22 §4.4]. There are three levels of error:
  - A serious error (one that could cause harm): he corrects it within 24 hours. He adds a dated note and sends an e-mail to subscribers.
  - A wrong fact: he corrects it within 72 hours.
  - A small error (a typing mistake or a broken link): he corrects it within 7 days.

  The log of corrections is public.
- **Test with ordinary listeners.** He tests every piece from a main programme with 3–5 listeners who are not medical. One must be over 60, and one must have schooling to Class 8 or less. A piece passes if 4 of 5 listeners can state the main message and the action.
- **Access for everyone.** Captions that a person has checked, written text on the website, image descriptions, audio-only versions, and large type [22 §4.7].
- **Wording on the limits of his advice.** He uses the honest wording in file 22 §4.6, in Telugu and English. It says what the content cannot do and what the reader should do next. He does not use a standard disclaimer that people ignore.

### 6.5 Who decides, and when publishing stops

**Who decides** [22 §6.2]:

- He decides every clinical claim and opinion.
- His friend decides the wording and the format, within the standard.
- The lawyer must read anything that names a company, a product, a practitioner, a hospital or a system of medicine.
- Either he or his friend may stop publishing. Only he may restart it.

**The 13 rules for when to stop publishing** [22 §6.3]. Publishing pauses when any of these happens:

1. A serious error.
2. A notice from the council.
3. A legal notice, or contact from the police.
4. A written objection from his employer.
5. A breach of confidentiality.
6. Someone takes over one of his accounts, or pretends to be him.
7. A leak of data.
8. An energy score of 2 or lower for two weeks, or four weeks above the limit on hours.

11. A rule changes and nobody has reviewed the change.

13. His friend is unavailable for more than two weeks, and there is no backup. He then falls to the **smallest amount of work**: one Telugu card every two weeks. He can do this alone in about 60 minutes a week.

**He has five short statements, approved in advance, for a first public reply** [22 §3.3]. They cover an error, a claim by a patient, a legal matter, someone pretending to be him, and a pause.

**He calls the lawyer within the same hour for:**

- any legal paper, call from the police or letter from the council;
- a journalist who asks about a named patient;
- a fake video or audio that is collecting money;
- a written objection from his employer;
- a leak of data;
- and, **before** he publishes it, anything that criticises a named company, product, hospital, practitioner or system of medicine.

## 8. How results are measured

**Main point:** he uses a small set of measures and always shows the total number behind each figure. **No measure anywhere counts new patients.** He judges reach each quarter and health results at 12 months or later [22 §5].

**Rules for claiming credit for results** [22 §5.3]:

- He claims that his work helped, not that it caused the result.
- "Heart School" compares its group with people on a waiting list.
- He never multiplies one measure through the next to claim a final result. For example, he never turns views into "heart attacks prevented".
- He counts people who leave a programme as not at goal.
- He publishes results that show no effect.

**Reach only shows how the work is going.** He records subscribers but does not set a target for them. He publishes one main video a week and two short clips taken from it. With this, an honest expectation is **3,000–10,000 subscribers at 12 months**. The research has low confidence in this figure [20 §6b].

**The quarterly review.** He and his friend spend 30 minutes on it. They use the one-page form in file 22 §5.4. They look first at any line that must never be crossed. Then they look at his workload, the three groups of people, the foundations and quality. They end with three decisions: what to stop, what to start and what to change. The review goes to the advisory group.

## 11. What any future AI system must do

This section lists requirements only. It does not design the system.

1. **Two separate paths.** The system for public teaching must be built so that it cannot receive patient data. Any clinical tool is separate and properly controlled. It works under a contract on data handling and with the patient's consent. It follows the data protection law (DPDP) from 13 May 2027 [18, 21, 30].
2. **A person approves every item.** Nothing is published or posted automatically without his logged approval of the final item, including its descriptions. The log records who approved, the time, the sources and the result of the checklist [11, 30 cl. 4.4].
3. **It drafts only from given sources.** It drafts from sources that he supplies and from his own words. It never supplies the references he relies on. It takes every claim into a fact list (each fact with its source) so that a person can check it [22 §4.5].
4. **It has compliance checks built in.**
   - It checks all text and descriptions against the list of warning words (in English and Telugu).
   - It inserts the credential line.
   - It refuses content with patient stories, reviews, words of superiority, booking details or brands.
   - It marks anything that names a company, a practitioner or a system of medicine for a lawyer's review [11 §9].
5. **It handles Telugu with care.** It treats Telugu speech recognition and translation only as drafts (25–29% of words wrong). It uses his approved list of about 150 terms. It sends every Telugu item to the human checker, with instructions translated back into English [23, 31].
6. **It makes no copy of him.** It makes no image of his face and no copy of his voice. It does not dub his voice. It makes no images of patients and offers no public chatbot. A future assistant may only find answers in the checked library. It must refuse personal medical questions. It must have a switch that turns it off at once, and a lawyer must approve it [18].
7. **It helps him state where AI was used.** It labels images that AI helped make or made, where this matters (clause 7.2(c)). It keeps a record of AI use for each item.
8. **It keeps logs and records.**
   - The log of corrections, with the time limits for serious errors, wrong facts and small errors.
   - The log of AI errors.
   - The record of income and of interests linked to industry.
   - The record of consent and of cases (case codes only).
   - The log of times when publishing stopped.
   - The data for the quarterly review.
9. **It tracks the calendar and deadlines.** Each month it checks dates that were estimated from earlier years against the organisers' websites. It tracks the next review date for every published piece.
10. **He owns everything, and others get only limited access.** He owns all accounts. His friend and the system use roles that he grants, not his passwords. (LinkedIn accounts cannot be shared.) The written instructions and the AI instructions are recorded. Then a backup person can keep the smallest amount of work going.
11. **It protects his time and energy.** It records his weekly hours and his energy score. It enforces the limit on time and the rules for when to stop publishing. It does not try to produce as much as possible.
12. **It enforces the rules on who may say what.** It tags each item by speaker and act (§4A). It blocks resharing, quoting or linking praise of him. It keeps private feedback out of every public item. The only exception is combined figures that identify nobody and that have already been published.
13. **It measures honestly.** It reports the total number behind each figure. It counts patients lost to follow-up as not at goal. It holds no measures that count new patients.
