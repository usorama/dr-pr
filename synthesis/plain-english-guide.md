# Plain-English guide for every document in this project

**Who reads these documents.** Dr. Avinash Pathuri and his friend. English is not their first language. Both are educated professionals; he is a cardiologist, his friend is a senior IT manager. They read carefully, but an idiom, a metaphor or a compressed phrase stops them. Write so that each sentence is understood the first time it is read.

## Sentence rules

1. One idea in one sentence. Aim for 12 to 18 words. Never more than 25.
2. Every sentence has a subject and a verb. No fragments. No lists of words joined by dots, slashes or semicolons where a sentence is needed.
3. Use the active voice and say who does it: "He signs the form", not "The form is signed".
4. Use the common word: "use" not "utilise"; "about" not "approximately"; "start" not "commence"; "help" not "facilitate"; "before" not "prior to"; "enough" not "sufficient"; "limit" not "cap"; "how often" not "cadence".
5. No idioms, no metaphors, no wordplay, no slogans. Examples to remove: "in the room", "bends", "lane", "funnel", "landmine", "bridge", "rented ground", "by-product", "door into", "down the ladder", "pile-on", "speaks through".
6. No "not X, but Y" slogans and no reveal after a colon. Say the point directly.
7. No dash asides. Use two sentences.
8. Do not turn verbs into nouns: "he decides" not "the decision is his"; "they agree" not "agreement is reached".
9. Prefer one plain verb to a phrasal verb when both exist: "remove" not "strike out"; "continue" not "carry on"; "begin" not "kick off".
10. No Latin or shorthand: write "for example", "that is", "through", "each", "against". Do not write e.g., i.e., via, per, vs.
11. Write dates in full: "4 December 2026". Write money as "Rs 4 lakh". Explain once that 1 lakh = 100,000.
12. When a percentage counts people, also say it as people: "27 of 100 patients".
13. Spell out every abbreviation the first time it appears in a document, then use the short form. Each document ends with a short list of abbreviations.
14. A heading is a plain phrase or sentence that says what the section tells the reader. No clever headings.
15. Start each section with its main point in one or two sentences.
16. Do not hedge with filler ("it is worth noting", "importantly", "in essence"). Do not praise the plan. State facts and instructions.

## Labels for legal points

Use these three labels, exactly, and explain them once at the top of each document.

| Old label | New label | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Text | **The rule says** | The words are in the rule itself. |
| Interpretation | **Our reading** | The rule does not say it in these words. This is how we read it. A lawyer should confirm. |
| Unsettled | **Not decided** | The rule does not answer the question, and no authority has answered it. |

In tables that show whether an act is allowed, write **Yes**, **Ask a lawyer** or **No**. Do not use single letters or colours as codes.

## What must not change

1. **Quotations from rules, laws and court orders stay exactly as they are**, inside quotation marks, with their clause numbers. They are written in legal English. After each quotation, add one line that begins "In plain words:" and gives the meaning. Do not make the plain line stronger or weaker than the quotation.
2. Every number, date, amount of money, percentage, name, web address and clause number stays the same.
3. Every source tag in square brackets, such as [24] or [31 §1.5], stays where it is.
4. Section numbers and the identifiers P1 to P11, O1 to O7 and R1 to R26 stay the same, so that other files can still point to them. The heading words may change.
5. Every fact, instruction, condition, warning and table row stays. Do not drop content to save space, and do not add new facts. If a sentence in the source is unclear, read the research file it cites and write what that file says.
6. Medical terms a cardiologist uses every day may stay (stent, angioplasty, statin, LDL cholesterol, heart failure). Explain them in a few words the first time, for his friend.
