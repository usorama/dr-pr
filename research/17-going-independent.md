# Going independent: scaling Dr. Avinash's own practice out of a corporate-hospital attachment, and sequencing the reputation plan around the exit

**Executive summary**

1. **He is not starting from zero.** Per the sponsor (Addendum 3) he already runs his own private practice alongside the Apollo attachment. Independence is therefore a *scale-up and re-weighting* problem: move the centre of gravity from the hospital's brand to a clinic entity he owns, and secure cath-lab access on terms he controls. The most urgent unknown is what his Apollo agreement says about outside practice.
2. **The law now leans his way on exit.** On 23 Feb 2026 the Madras High Court (*MIOT Hospitals v. Dr Balaraman Palaniappan*) held a 3-year / 15-km non-compete and non-solicitation clause in a doctor-hospital agreement void under Sections 23 and 27 of the Contract Act, with costs against the hospital. It is persuasive, not binding, in Telangana; notice periods, confidentiality and in-term exclusivity remain enforceable.
3. **The regulator treats his own clinic more generously than an employer.** A registered clinic owned by "a single doctor" is a clinical establishment (Clinical Establishments Act 2010, s.2(c)), so it falls inside the 2026 Guidelines' "Hospital/Medical Institution" lane: factual name, location, contact, facilities, equipment and fees may be published (cl. 8.3(i), 9.1; 2002 Reg. 7.12), plus formal announcements (cl. 8.2(ii), 9.3) and a signboard (Reg. 7.13). Self-promotion, testimonials, superiority claims and review requests stay barred.
4. **Build only on assets he owns.** Hospital profile pages, hospital videos and reviews on the hospital's Google listing do not travel. His name domain, website, newsletter list, YouTube/LinkedIn/X accounts, publications, society memberships and a Google Business Profile at his own clinic address do. Patient lists held by the hospital are the hospital's data and must not be copied.
5. **Recommended shape (logic, not prescription):** own outpatient heart clinic as the hub + non-exclusive procedural privileges at two hospitals, with a prevention/rehab/tele layer added in year 1; defer owning a cath lab until volumes justify it. The decision table, the pre-exit / exit / post-exit checklist and 20 questions for a lawyer are in section 8.

### 2. Exit mechanics and legal constraints

#### 2.1 Typical consultant agreement terms

From the one agreement whose terms are public through litigation (*MIOT*, Chennai): three-year term; three months' notice or payment of three months' professional fees in lieu (cl. 10.2); confidentiality, non-solicitation and non-compete (cl. 8), the last, as reported, barring the doctor for three years after termination from joining a "rival hospital" or setting up practice within a 15-kilometre radius; liquidated damages (₹42 lakh claimed). These are press paraphrases of the clauses, not the clause text. The court itself suspected "copy paste syndrome", which suggests such clauses are boilerplate across chains. Confidence: High that such clauses are common; **his own Apollo agreement has not been seen.**

#### 2.2 Holding an own practice while still attached to Apollo (in-term exclusivity and conflict risk)

- **Law.** Section 27 voids agreements in restraint of a lawful profession, but Indian courts have long distinguished restraints that operate *during* the term of an engagement (generally enforceable if reasonable; *Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning*, SC 1967) from restraints *after* it ends (void; *Superintendence Co. v. Krishan Murgai*, SC 1980; *Percept D'Mark v. Zaheer Khan*, SC 2006). These three are cited from prior legal knowledge, not retrieved this session. *MIOT* dealt only with post-termination restraint; the reports "do not state whether restraints during the contract term are enforceable". **So an exclusivity or "no outside practice" clause in a current agreement is probably enforceable while the agreement runs.** Confidence: Medium.
- **Typical positions.** Full-time/MG consultants are often exclusive to the hospital (sometimes with a carve-out for a named clinic or for the group's own satellite clinics, which is what "Apollo Clinic Narsingi" looks like). Visiting/FFS consultants are usually non-exclusive. A sample retainer template found in search requires the doctor to "use their personal letterheads and stationary for their home practice and in no case, use the letterheads or logo or any other intellectual property right of the Company or the Hospital", i.e. it presupposes a separate home practice with branding kept apart (template, not authority). Confidence: Low-Medium.
- **Conflict risks while both run in parallel:**
  1. *Undisclosed outside practice* in breach of an exclusivity clause: grounds for termination, withholding of dues, or a damages claim.
  2. *Patient diversion ("leakage")*: moving hospital-registered patients to his own clinic for consultations or tests. Even without a clause this invites a solicitation complaint (Reg. 6.1.1) and sours the reference he will need for privileges elsewhere.
  3. *Use of hospital resources*: staff time, stationery, the hospital's EMR/patient contacts, hospital logo or photos on own-clinic material.
  4. *Brand confusion*: own-clinic listing or signboard that mentions Apollo; a Google profile for the own clinic sitting at the hospital's address.
  5. *Procedures*: patients seen at his own clinic and taken for PCI to a hospital other than Apollo while he is on an exclusive or MG arrangement.
  6. *Media clause*: many agreements require prior approval for public statements that name the hospital; none found for Apollo (file 13).
- **Practical rule until the contract is read by a lawyer:** keep the two practices visibly separate (own letterhead, own phone, own records, own listing, no Apollo name), disclose the own practice in writing if the agreement requires disclosure, and do not move a single hospital-registered patient on his own initiative.

#### 2.3 Post-termination non-compete and non-solicitation

- ***MIOT Hospitals Pvt Ltd v. Dr Balaraman Palaniappan***, Madras High Court, Justice N. Anand Venkatesh, 23 Feb 2026 (Arb. O.P. (Com. Div.) No. 708 of 2025; 2026 SCC OnLine Mad 1756; 2026 LiveLaw (Mad) 85). A consultant cardiothoracic surgeon gave three months' notice and joined Apollo Speciality Hospital; MIOT sought an arbitrator and ₹42 lakh. Held: such clauses are "certainly opposed to public policy and such an agreement is squarely hit by Section 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872", also void under Section 27, "void ab initio"; a doctor is "an independent professional, who cannot be stopped from rendering his services wherever he wants to"; "Doctors can thrive without hospitals whereas a hospital can never exist without doctors"; petition dismissed with ₹1 lakh costs for an attempt "to witch-hunt a doctor". During the hearing the judge remarked "The client can go to the person whom he trusts" (Medical Dialogues, 18 Feb 2026). Confidence: High.
- **Limits of the ruling.** Single-judge decision of another High Court: persuasive in Telangana, not binding. No appeal was mentioned in any report; **appeal status not verified**. The decision turned partly on the doctor having served full notice. Reports are unclear whether the confidentiality clause was separately struck down.
- **What remains enforceable in practice:** the notice period or fees in lieu; return of hospital property and data; confidentiality of hospital business information and patient data; recovery of specific advances (sign-on, training bond, MG shortfall if the contract says so) is a question for the lawyer; arbitration clauses.
- **Non-solicitation.** *MIOT* treated it as void in a doctor contract. Elsewhere Indian courts have sometimes enforced narrowly drawn non-solicit covenants while refusing to stop customers who come of their own accord (prior knowledge; Medium). Independently of contract, *active* solicitation of patients is professional misconduct under Reg. 6.1.1 and Guideline 8.1(ii). **So: patients may follow him; he may not go and fetch them.**

#### 2.4 Who owns patient records and contact data

- **Regulation 1.3.1:** every physician "shall maintain the medical records pertaining to his / her indoor patients for a period of 3 years". **1.3.2:** on request by the patient, authorised attendant or legal authorities, "documents shall be issued within the period of 72 hours". The patient's right to a copy is the lawful portability mechanism: a patient who chooses to follow him can obtain records from the hospital and bring them.
- **Hospital records** (EMR, cath-lab images, device data, phone numbers in the hospital information system) are held by the hospital as custodian and, under the DPDP Act 2023, as the data fiduciary that determined the purpose and means of processing. Commentary treats "every hospital, clinic, diagnostic lab" as a data fiduciary (Kratikal). No source found that addresses a departing doctor directly; the following is inference (Medium): exporting a patient list from the hospital system for use in his own practice would be processing for a new purpose without consent, a breach of the confidentiality clause, and evidence of solicitation.
- **His own clinic's records** are his; he is the fiduciary and carries the 3-year retention duty and, from 13 May 2027, DPDP notice and consent duties.
- **Phone contacts.** Patients and families who have his number, or whose numbers he holds because they contacted him directly or through his own clinic, are his own relationships. Numbers known only through the hospital's system are not.

#### 2.6 The regulator's rules on formal announcements (quoted)

- **2002 Regulations, 6.1.1:** "A medical practitioner is however permitted to make a formal announcement in press regarding the following: (1) On starting practice. (2) On change of type of practice. (3) On changing address. (4) On temporary absence from duty. (5) On resumption of another practice. (6) On succeeding to another practice. (7) Public declaration of charges."
- **2026 Guidelines, 8.2(ii):** "A medical practitioner is however permitted to make a formal announcement regarding the following: a) On starting practice. b) On change of type of practice. c) On changing address. d) On temporary absence from duty. e) On resumption of another practice. f) On succeeding to another practice. g) Public declaration of charge."
- **2026 Guidelines, 9.3:** "RMP can issue a formal announcement which are factual, non-promotional and limited in scope regarding starting of practice, change of address, change of practice type, temporary absence, resumption of practice, public declaration of consultation fees."
- **Medium.** The 2002 text says "in press". The 2026 text drops "in press" and its definition of media (3.5) covers websites, social media and messaging platforms, so a factual announcement on his own website, profile and channels is defensible. Confidence: Medium-High (interpretation).
- **Frequency.** Neither text sets a number. The only numeric limit ever written was in the 2023 Regulations (Reg. 11(A): "within 3 months"), which are in abeyance. For hospitals the 2026 text warns that "the form, medium or frequency of communication shall not convert otherwise factual information into solicitation or promotional self-aggrandizement" (8.3(ii)). **Working rule: one announcement per event, per channel, within three months of the event, never repeated as a campaign, never boosted with paid reach.**
- **Content.** Name, recognised qualifications, speciality, registration number with council, new address, effective date, consultation hours, phone, fees. No photograph (Reg. 6.1.2; cl. 9.2), no procedure counts, no "formerly of Apollo" used as a credential (see 7.3), no adjectives.
- His move is two permitted events at once: "change of type of practice" (hospital-attached to independent) and "changing address".

#### 2.7 Patients mid-treatment (continuity-of-care duties)

- **Reg. 2.4:** "Once having undertaken a case, the physician should not neglect the patient, nor should he withdraw from the case without giving adequate notice to the patient and his family."
- **Reg. 2.1.1:** "in case of emergency a physician must treat the patient".
- **Reg. 3.6:** when a patient is referred, "a case summary of the patient should be given to the specialist". **Reg. 4.4** governs substitutes during absence.
- **Cardiology-specific handover list** (practice judgment): patients within 12 months of PCI on dual antiplatelets; staged PCI awaiting second stage; pacemaker/ICD/CRT patients due for interrogation (the programmer and data usually stay with the hospital); heart-failure patients mid-titration; patients on anticoagulation with INR follow-up; in-patients on the day he leaves. For each: a written summary, a named successor at the hospital, and a clear statement that the patient is free to choose where to continue.

### 3. What an existing own practice already permits

This is the lane available **today**, before any exit, subject to section 2.2 (contract) and the registration prerequisite.

#### 3.1 The legal hook: his clinic is itself a "clinical establishment"

- **Clinical Establishments Act 2010, s.2(c):** "clinical establishment" means "(i) a hospital, maternity home, nursing home, dispensary, clinic, sanatorium or an institution by whatever name called that offers services, facilities requiring diagnosis, treatment or care for illness…" and "shall include a clinical establishment owned, controlled or managed by— … (e) a single doctor". (Gazette text read.)
- **2026 Guidelines, 3.4:** "Hospital/Medical Institution" means "a clinical establishment providing facilities and services for the diagnosis, treatment or care of illness… where patients may be admitted and treated as in-patients or treated as out-patients… and includes an institution covered under Section 2(c)(i) of the Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010".
- **Reading.** A registered outpatient clinic owned by one doctor is therefore within the Guidelines' institutional lane as well as the RMP lane. This is the more permissive lawful reading, and the text supports it. It is still interpretation: the Guidelines also say "Nothing contained in these Guidelines shall be construed as permitting any act which is prohibited under the applicable law or the aforesaid Regulations" (2.2). Confidence: Medium-High.

### 4. What is portable and what is not

#### 4.1 Google Business Profile (the most valuable and least understood asset)

- **Google's guideline (read this session):** "An individual practitioner is a public-facing professional, typically with their own customer base." A practitioner should create a dedicated profile if "They operate in a public-facing role" and "They can be contacted directly at the verified location during stated hours." "If the practitioner is one of several public-facing practitioners at this location: The organization should create a Business Profile for this location, separate from that of the practitioner. The title of the Business Profile for the practitioner should include only the name of the practitioner". "A practitioner shouldn't have multiple Business Profiles to cover all of their specializations." For a solo practitioner representing a brand, "it's best for the practitioner to share a Business Profile with the organization" in the format "[brand/company]: [practitioner name]". Confidence: High.
- **Google's guideline is silent on what happens when a practitioner leaves.** I checked; the page does not address it. What follows is consistent expert practice, not Google policy:
  - Joy Hawkins (Sterling Sky; Google Product Expert): "what you can do with the listing often comes down to who has control of the listing. If the brand/company controls the listing, they are able to get the listing deleted from Google if they specifically request it… If the practitioner is the one that controls the listing, they have the ability to update the listing to list the details of the new place they work."
  - V Digital Services (updated June 2026): "Reviews on practitioner listings stay with the practitioner across job changes"; "reviews on the firm listing stay with the location."
  - Local Search Forum moderators (2019, 2025): "the practitioner listing belong to the individual and they can take the listing with them"; others call review transfers "a gray area", and one practice reported Google deleting reviews after a merge. Confidence: Medium.
- **Ownership requests:** a profile verified by someone else can be requested through business.google.com; the current owner "has 3 days to respond", after which a claim option may appear (not always). The current owner is notified by email. Confidence: High.
- **Implications for him:**
  1. Where his "strong Google reviews" sit is still unknown (file 12). Three cases: (a) on a practitioner listing in his name that he owns: portable, he edits the address on exit; (b) on a practitioner listing the hospital's team created and owns: he should obtain primary ownership well before notice, since the owner can have it deleted; (c) on the Apollo Financial District hospital listing: not portable at all.
  2. **The own clinic should have its own profile at its own address, on his own Google account.** Reviews that accrue there are immune to any exit. One listing per physical location where he actually keeps hours (file 10).
  3. He may not ask patients to review or move reviews (Explanation V). Reviews must arrive unprompted.
  4. Changing a listing's address can trigger re-verification; do it once, after the move is real.

### 5. Sequencing the reputation plan around the exit

Let **N** = the day notice is given and **T** = the last day at Apollo (N + notice period, probably about 90 days; to be read from the contract).

#### 5.1 Before notice (now to N)

Build, quietly and in his own name:
1. **Canonical name and registration line** fixed everywhere (file 12).
2. **Entity home:** name domain + a factual, education-first website with an "About" page that does not depend on any hospital (hospital names appear only as "consults at").
3. **Own-clinic factual layer:** CE registration current; signboard and fee board compliant; clinic Google profile verified on his account; Practo/Justdial entries for the clinic correct (section 3).
4. **Newsletter list** started (education only).
5. **Owned channels** live with a first body of educational content, so that the day he leaves nothing visibly "starts".
6. **Peer network:** CSI/TGCSI activity, case presentations, teaching sessions for trainees; relationships with cath-lab directors and physician-owners at two or three hospitals (as colleagues, not yet as negotiations).
7. **Publications** submitted with an affiliation line that will survive ("Consultant Interventional Cardiologist, Hyderabad, India" plus current institution as required by the journal).
8. **Community programmes** in his own name or under a neutral banner.
9. **Clinical log** of his own procedures and outcomes (de-identified, for credentialing applications at other hospitals, not for publicity).

Avoid before notice:
- Anything that reads as soliciting the hospital's patients: telling patients "I am leaving, come to my clinic", collecting numbers in the OPD, messaging hospital-derived contacts.
- Copying any hospital data.
- Hospital resources for own-clinic work.
- Public hints about leaving, or criticism of corporate medicine tied to his employer.
- Signing with another hospital on terms that breach current exclusivity.
- Putting effort into hospital-hosted content.

#### 5.2 Notice period (N to T)

- Serve the full notice or pay in lieu exactly as the contract says (*MIOT* turned on this). Keep a paper trail.
- Hand over patients formally (section 2.7). Ask the hospital in writing how patients who ask for him will be answered.

- Finalise privileges at the new hospitals (credentialing committees take weeks).
- Do not announce publicly until the relationship has ended, unless the hospital agrees.

#### 5.3 The announcement window (T to T + 90 days)

- One factual announcement per channel (section 2.6): a small press insertion in one English and one Telugu daily; the website; the clinic's Google profile (hours/address); directory updates; one plain post on each owned account; the newsletter.
- Update every listing in the order: own site, Google profiles, Practo, society directories, ORCID/Scholar/LinkedIn, then third-party directories; ask Apollo to remove or correct his page (keep the request log, as with Citizens).
- No paid reach, no "launch event" with patient stories, no inauguration publicity built around his photograph. A quiet academic opening (a CME for local physicians) is consistent with the rules and with his temperament.

#### 5.5 How the "do not promote your employer" clause works in his favour

- Hospitals may no longer run "promotional portrayal of an individual RMP" (8.3(ii)), which blunts the usual advantage of chain-employed peers who are promoted by a marketing department. The field tilts toward doctors with their own teaching voice.
- After the exit there is no employer. The platform is his name; the content rules (5.1) are identical to what he was already following, so nothing about his public voice has to change. That continuity is itself the strategy.
- Caution: if he takes an MG/retainer at another hospital (model B), that hospital becomes an "employer organization" again.

- **Signboard, letterhead, fee board:** as in 3.2.
- **Website and directories:** name of clinic; his name, recognised qualifications, speciality, registration; address, hours, phone; services and equipment stated factually (Explanation II); fees; languages; accreditation if any. Education articles separately.

### 6. A referral economy without unethical practice

#### 6.1 The prohibition (quoted)

- **Reg. 6.4.1:** "A physician shall not give, solicit, or receive nor shall he offer to give solicit or receive, any gift, gratuity, commission or bonus in consideration of or return for the referring, recommending or procuring of any patient for medical, surgical or other treatment. A physician shall not directly or indirectly, participate in or be a party to act of division, transference, assignment, subordination, rebating, splitting or refunding of any fee".
- **Reg. 6.4.2** extends this to diagnostics, and adds: "Nothing in this section, however, shall prohibit payment of salaries by a qualified physician to other duly qualified person rendering medical care under his supervision."
- **Reg. 7.19:** no "touts or agents for procuring patients". **2026 cl. 8.1(vii)–(viii):** no consideration "connected with the referral, recommendation or procurement of patients"; agency deals must not be "linked to the procurement or referral of individual patients".
- **Reality check.** The UNU-IIGH paper (Sept 2026) reports clinicians saying "it is routine for specialists to share a portion of their fees with referring doctors". He will be asked. A stated no-cuts policy will cost some referrals early and is also the cleanest differentiator he can have with the physicians worth working with.
- This also governs his own hospital deals: his share must be a professional fee for work done, never a percentage for "bringing" a patient to a facility, and he must not take commissions from labs, imaging centres or pharmacies.

#### 6.2 Ethical referral-building (what independent specialists actually do)

| Practice | How | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| **Same-day referral letter and feedback** (Reg. 3.6 makes the written opinion a duty) | One-page letter to the referring doctor: diagnosis, what was done, plan, when to send back. Return the patient to the referrer for ongoing care (Reg. 4.3: consultant "should normally not take charge of the case"). | 5 min/patient with a template |
| **CME for GPs and physicians** | Small-group sessions on ECG reading, chest-pain triage, lipid and BP management, when to refer. Avoid pharma-paid hospitality (UCPMP; file 02). | 2–3 h/month |
| **Shared-care protocols** | Written protocols for post-PCI follow-up, heart failure titration, AF anticoagulation, so the GP keeps the patient. | One-off 6–10 h, then updates |
| **Second-opinion service** | Structured review of angiograms/reports with a written opinion; no criticism of the first doctor (Reg. 4.2). | 30–45 min/case |
| **Direct access for doctors** | A doctors-only phone/WhatsApp line for ECG queries and urgent transfers. | Ongoing |
| **STEMI/chest-pain pathway** with nearby nursing homes | Agreed transfer protocol to the hospital where he has privileges. | One-off, then drills |
| **Teaching** | DNB/DM trainee teaching, case conferences. Builds the future referral network honestly. | 2–4 h/month |

#### 6.3 Insurance, TPA, Aarogyasri and CGHS realities

- **Cashless private insurance** is contracted between insurers/TPAs and *hospitals*; an outpatient clinic is generally outside indemnity cover, and procedures are billed under the hospital's network status. So his choice of procedural hospitals determines what his patients' insurance will pay. (General industry knowledge; Medium.)
- **Aarogyasri (Telangana):** the network has about 1,042 hospitals, 409 of them private (search-result figure). Payment is the problem: "Over Rs 2,000 crore" of bills pending for 11–15 months; none of 360 small and mid-sized private hospitals had signed the revamped Employee Health Scheme MoU; one procedure package fell from ₹32,100 to ₹26,500 (Telangana Today, 25 Sep 2026). Empanelment criteria (beds, specialties) **could not be verified**; the Trust's site returned no content. An individual doctor does not empanel; the hospital does.
- **CGHS:** hospital-level empanelment; criteria not verified this session. Reports say major Hyderabad private hospitals have high refusal rates for Aarogyasri, PM-JAY and CGHS patients.
- **Implication:** scheme work is a hospital-partner question, not a clinic question. If serving scheme patients matters to him (it fits the "for everyone" principle), one of his procedural hospitals should be empanelled, and model D (own centre) carries real receivables risk.

#### 7.2 Trademark basics (India)

- Services of a medical clinic fall in **Class 44**; education and training in **Class 41**. Registration lasts 10 years and is renewable.
- Do a public search on the IP India register before choosing; avoid descriptive names ("Hyderabad Heart Clinic") that cannot be protected.
- A word mark for the coined name plus a simple logo is enough at this stage. Register the matching domains and handles the same day.
- The trademark, domain, Google profile and bank account should all be held by him or his own entity, never by an agency, landlord, partner hospital or the friend.

#### 7.3 A founder story that is truthful and non-promotional

- **Use:** why he practises the way he does (time with patients, prevention before procedures, teaching), what the clinic does (factual), where he trained (recognised qualifications), what he is studying or teaching now.
- **Do not use:** "left corporate medicine to give patients honest care" (comparative and disparaging; cl. 8.1(ix), Reg. 4.2), procedure counts or outcomes (8.1(iii)), patient stories (6.2), "formerly at Apollo" as a headline credential (borrows a brand he no longer has and may breach post-exit IP terms; stating past appointments factually in a CV section is fine).

## Risks & things to avoid

- **Assuming the own practice is contractually safe.** In-term exclusivity is generally enforceable; an undisclosed parallel practice is the most likely trigger for a dispute.
- **Taking or using hospital patient data.** Contract breach, confidentiality breach, likely DPDP exposure, and proof of solicitation in one act.
- **Soliciting.** Telling hospital patients to follow him, mass messages, inducements, or an "opening offer". Patients may follow; he may not fetch.
- **Treating *MIOT* as a shield for everything.** It is one single-judge ruling from another state, on post-termination restraints, where the doctor had served notice.
- **Using the institutional lane as a back door.** A solo clinic's "factual information" must not become a portrait of the doctor's achievements (cl. 8.3(ii), Reg. 6.1.1).
- **Announcement creep.** Repeating the "new clinic" notice, boosting it, or dressing an inauguration as news.
- **Review requests** and any use of patient stories at launch.
- **Building on rented ground**: hospital pages, a listing the hospital owns, accounts registered to an agency or the friend.
- **Exclusive arrangements with a new hospital** that recreate the dependence he is leaving.
- **Buying a cath lab early.** Debt and scheme receivables would dictate his clinical and public behaviour.
- **Cuts and commissions**, including disguised ones (percentage "facility" kickbacks, lab commissions, paid lead-generation).
- **Borrowing Apollo's name** after exit in headlines, listings or the clinic's Google profile.
- **Criticising corporate hospitals** as a positioning device. It is comparative, invites retaliation, and is off-character.
- **Uncorrected stale listings** (Citizens shows how long they linger).
