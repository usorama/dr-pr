# Earned media, public writing, speaking and books — how an Indian cardiologist becomes the "go-to expert source"

**Research date:** 12 November 2026. **Subject:** Dr. Avinash Paturi / "Dr. Pathuri Avinash", interventional cardiologist, Hyderabad.

## Executive summary

1. **The rulebook changed five weeks ago.** NMC's "Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication" (6 Oct 2026, in force immediately) now list "public interviews" and "news articles intended for promotion" as advertising media, bar engaging "any third party for marketing medical services", and allow public-health talks on TV/radio/electronic media only "in their own name and designation, without promoting their employer organization". Every earned-media tactic below has to pass that test. (High confidence, primary source read in full.)
2. **Earned media in Hyderabad is supply-driven.** Local cardiology coverage is dominated by hospital-issued "rare procedure / patient saved" stories and seasonal explainers quoting whoever is reachable; reporters repeatedly reuse the same few experts. A doctor who answers fast, in plain language, with 40–60-word quotes and no sales pitch fills a real gap. (Medium.)
3. **The cheapest, safest route is education-first, not agency-first:** a 25–40 name journalist list built from bylines, a pre-written "peg bank" tied to a fixed annual calendar (winter MI season, World Heart Day 29 Sept, ACC/ESC/TCT/AHA/CSI trial weeks, Hyderabad Marathon), and one bylined piece a month. Estimated 60–90 minutes a week of the doctor's time once set up.
4. **Speaking and books are slow, compounding assets.** TEDxHyderabad starts speaker selection 8–10 months ahead and accepts open nominations; Manthan, Hyderabad Literary Festival, Hyderabad Runners (Apollo is its medical partner), HYSEA and TiE are realistic local stages. A trade book takes roughly 18–30 months through Penguin/Rupa/Pan Macmillan/Juggernaut (all take emailed proposals); a Telugu heart-health handbook is a faster 3–4 month first step.
5. **Research caveat:** the shared web-search budget for this session ran out after about 25 queries, and The Hindu, Indian Express, Times of India, Hindustan Times, ET HealthWorld, NDTV, News18, Moneycontrol, New Indian Express, HarperCollins, Bloomsbury and Apollo's own site blocked page fetches. Items marked **[R]** are recalled from background knowledge and were *not* verified this round; treat them as leads to check, not facts.

### 0. The regulatory frame that governs everything in this report

Primary source: NMC Ethics & Medical Registration Board public notice No. R-13014/01/2024-Ethics dated 06/10/2026, signed by the NMC Secretary, "shall come into force with immediate effect" — 13-page PDF read in full (hosted by Medical Dialogues). **[V] High.**

Clauses that bear directly on earned media, speaking, books and awards:

| Clause | What it says (close paraphrase or short quote) | Consequence for this plan |
|---|---|---|
| 3.2 + Expl. I | "Advertisement" = any communication that directly or indirectly promotes an RMP's "professional services, reputation, skills, qualifications, achievements". Exempt: information to the public "without same being promotional and without any intent for commercial benefit". | The test is *promotional character*, not the channel. Education passes; "why I am good" does not. |
| 3.5 | "Medium of advertisement" expressly includes podcasts, "public interviews", "news articles intended for promotion", messaging platforms, AI-generated promotion. | An interview or planted news article *can* be an advertisement. |
| Expl. III | An RMP publishing electronic media posts should disclose name, qualifications, registration status and SMR/NMR registration number. | Add the Telangana medical council registration number to bios, bylines, slide footers, video descriptions. |
| 5.1 | Doctors "may participate in health awareness programmers, public health campaigns, academic discussions, educational content" provided these "do not promote personal practice, do not solicit patients and are not monetized through promotional marketing". | The positive permission the whole plan rests on. |
| 8.1(ii) | Shall not solicit patients "directly or indirectly", promote services "through self-promotion", or "engage any third party for marketing medical services". | A PR agency retained to *market him* is on the wrong side. A communications helper for public education is defensible only if the output is non-promotional. |
| 8.1(iii), (ix) | No advertising of personal achievements, success rates, patient numbers; no "best", "No.1", "leading", "top" unless objectively verifiable with transparent methodology. Any ranking or award relied upon must be independently verifiable with methodology and awarding body disclosed. | Paid "top doctor" lists and trophies cannot be used. Procedure counts ("2,300 angiographies") should not be pushed in PR copy. |
| 8.1(v) | Shall not "publish cases for promotional purposes", "publicize celebrity patients", "promote personal success stories" unless strictly scientific/educational with anonymised patient consent. | The standard hospital "rare case saved by Dr X" press release is now a personal risk for the named doctor. |
| 8.2(i) | "Only research-based communication, wherein they have published interesting case studies in reputed medical journals shall be allowed." | Publish first in a journal, then talk about it. |
| 8.2(iii) | Doctors "may deliver lectures or talks on public health and diseases for public awareness through television, radio and electronic media in their own name and designation, without promoting their employer organization." | Appear as "Dr Avinash Paturi, interventional cardiologist". Hospital branding in the appearance is the grey zone. |
| 7.2 | AI-generated promotional campaigns for commercial interest are prohibited; compliant AI-generated content must carry a mark stating its origin is AI. | Matters for the planned Claude-assisted system: AI drafts must be educational, doctor-reviewed, and labelled where the AI origin is material. |
| 9.4 | Healthcare platforms must ensure "prohibition of paid ranking of doctors". | Reinforces avoiding paid listings. |
| 10.1, 11.5 | Graded penalties: warning + ethics training → censure + fine → 3–6 month suspension → up to removal for 1–3 years. An RMP who "personally authorized, participated in, endorsed" a hospital's prohibited communication can be examined separately. | "The hospital's PR team did it" is not a defence. |

Corroboration: The South First (byline Sumit Jha, 7 Oct 2026) and Medical Dialogues (9 Oct 2026) reported the same provisions. **[V] High.**

Older rule still in force alongside: Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations 2002 say a physician should not contribute lay-press articles or give interviews that have the effect of advertising or soliciting, but may write in the lay press under his own name on public health — as summarised in Outlook's reporting. I could not open the regulation text itself on nmc.org.in (404). **[V via secondary source] Medium.** The August 2023 NMC conduct regulations, which first addressed social media, were withdrawn after IMA protests (ThePrint, 1 Apr 2024). **[V] High.**

**Could not verify:** any IMA or Telangana Medical Council reaction to, or clarification of, the October 2026 guidelines.

**1.2 How reporters find doctor sources**

- **Hospital PR is the main pipe.** Telangana Today's cardiology coverage is mostly hospital announcements; a November 2025 CARE Hospitals cardiogenic-shock story carried no reporter byline, one named hospital cardiologist, no independent voice. **[V] High.**
- **PR agencies pitch ready-made stories with doctor quotes.** Outlook's investigation described agencies sending "story ideas" and releases; it reported doctors paying Rs 25,000–75,000 to be mentioned about five times a month, and one release printed almost verbatim under a reporter's byline. The Indian Journal of Medical Ethics' Sandhya Srinivasan called the practice a violation of the MCI code. **[V] Medium — page is dated Feb 2022 but the reporting appears older; treat fees as stale.**
- **WhatsApp is the working channel.** An IJNet piece on Indian health reporting says messaging platforms made it easier to reach experts quickly, that many experts will only speak off the record, and that some reporters quote the same experts in every story. **[V] Medium — 2018, possibly stale.**
- **Wire copy travels.** A winter-heart-attack explainer in Telangana Today was IANS copy quoting Delhi doctors (one of them a neurointerventionist presented as a cardiologist). **[V] High.** Implication: a Hyderabad cardiologist is not competing with many local voices on seasonal explainers.
- **X/LinkedIn discovery and lifestyle desks writing stories off doctors' social posts**: widely observed, but **[R] Low** this round.
- **Expert databases:** no India-specific doctor-expert database could be verified. PR-side media databases exist (Wizikey markets a media database and monitoring; its public "health-tech journalists" list was mostly non-health names, so quality is doubtful). **[V] Medium.**

**1.3 Building a journalist list ethically**

No source prescribes this; it is practitioner guidance (Medium):

1. For four weeks, log every byline on a heart/health story in the target outlets (start with the verified names in 1.1). Record name, outlet, beat, last three stories, public contact route (author page, X handle, LinkedIn).
2. Tier it: Tier A = 8–10 Hyderabad/Telangana health reporters; Tier B = 10–15 national health reporters and health-desk editors; Tier C = Telugu print/TV health-page editors and producers.
3. First contact is a one-paragraph introduction, not a pitch: who he is, registration number, three topics he can explain, "reachable on WhatsApp between X and Y, I will reply within the hour or say I can't".
4. Never: buy lists, add reporters to broadcast groups without consent, offer gifts/hospitality/free check-ups, ask to "see the copy", or send hospital-branded releases about his own cases.
5. Review quarterly; reporters change beats often (note the unverifiable names above).

**1.4 Response-time expectations**

Could not find a published Indian norm. Practitioner guidance (Medium–Low): digital desks working a breaking peg want a usable quote within 30–60 minutes; print reporters filing for next-day editions generally need it by late afternoon; features and explainers allow 24–48 hours. A fast "can't today, try Dr Y" preserves the relationship better than silence.

**1.5 Quote-ready commentary**

Verified datum: in the IANS winter explainer, each direct quote ran roughly 40–58 words, two per doctor. **[V] High.** So the unit of work is small.

Template for every peg (sent as WhatsApp text, not PDF):
- Line 1 — what happened / what the study found, in one plain sentence.
- Line 2 — what it means for an ordinary Indian adult.
- Line 3 — one action or one caution ("this does not mean…").
- Attribution line: "Dr Avinash Paturi, MD, DM (Cardiology), interventional cardiologist, Hyderabad. Reg. No. ___."
- Optional: one Telugu version of the same three lines.

Peg types and the standing position to hold ready:

| Peg | Safe, useful angle | Hard rule |
|---|---|---|
| New trial (ACC/ESC/TCT/AHA) | What changes for patients on Monday; what does not | Disclose if he was an investigator or has industry ties |
| Celebrity cardiac event | General explainer: heart attack vs cardiac arrest, warning signs, CPR | Never speculate on the individual's diagnosis; never mention own patients (8.1(v)) |
| Sudden death of a young person / gym / badminton | Screening who actually needs it; avoiding panic testing | No fear-based framing (8.1(i)) |
| Heat wave | Dehydration, diuretics/BP drugs, outdoor workers | — |
| Air pollution | PM2.5 and cardiac events; who should limit exertion | — |
| Winter / festival season | Morning BP surge, salt and alcohol load, missed medicines | — |
| World Heart Day, World Hypertension Day, CPR/Restart a Heart, Doctors' Day | One fresh local data point or one myth corrected | Not a hospital "package" offer (8.1(x)) |

**4.3 Corporate wellness talks, RWAs, schools, clubs** — no citable source found for how Hyderabad IT companies book doctors; the following is practitioner knowledge (Medium–Low): requests typically come from HR/benefits or employee-wellness teams, often via the hospital's corporate-relations desk or the insurer/wellness vendor; the standard format is 30–45 minutes plus Q&A at lunchtime, increasingly hybrid; peak demand is World Heart Day week and after any in-company cardiac death. **4.5 One-sheet, reel, and talk design for a non-performer** (practitioner guidance, Medium)

- One-sheet (single page): 60-word bio, registration number, three talk titles each with a one-line promise and audience, formats/lengths/languages, two photos, contact (the friend, not the doctor).
- Reel: 90–120 seconds cut from the first three real talks. Do not stage one.
- Talk design that suits an introvert: one talk reused many times rather than many talks; structure of three stories and one ask; open with a case (anonymised, composite), not a joke; no more than 10 slides, mostly pictures; a physical demonstration (hands-only CPR on a manikin) to replace charisma with usefulness; scripted first and last 60 seconds; Q&A as the centrepiece, since answering questions is his clinic skill; TED's own preparation ladder (outline at 6 months, script at 5, rehearsals from 4) as the model for big stages.

**7.1 Agency or freelancer?**

- Only fee evidence found: Rs 25,000–75,000 for roughly five media mentions a month per doctor, from Outlook's reporting on Delhi agencies. **[V] but stale; Hyderabad 2026 retainers could not be verified.**

- Conclusion: a per-mention PR retainer is both the costliest and the riskiest option. The defensible structure is an unpaid or flat-fee communications helper (the friend) doing logistics for genuinely educational output, with the doctor approving every word.

**7.2 DIY toolkit** (practitioner guidance): a journalist sheet (1.3); Google Alerts for his name in both spellings and for "heart attack Hyderabad"; a peg bank of 20 pre-approved three-line comments; the press kit (6); a log of every media contact and appearance; a WhatsApp Business line run by the friend for media only.

**7.3 Press releases** — for an individual doctor, almost never. Legitimate triggers: a peer-reviewed publication (permitted, 8.2(i)), a public event such as a free CPR training, or a factual announcement of the permitted kinds (starting practice, change of address, etc., 8.2(ii)). Format when used: 300–400 words, dateline, one quote, no superlatives, registration number, contact. Case-based "patient saved" releases should be declined (8.1(v)).

**7.4 Crisis communication** — no India-specific authoritative guide was retrieved; the following is practitioner guidance to be validated with a medico-legal adviser (Medium–Low):

| Situation | First 24 hours | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Adverse-outcome allegation (family, social media, TV) | Inform hospital medical administration, legal and corporate communications, and the professional indemnity insurer before saying anything. Secure records. One holding line only: sympathy, confirmation that the matter is being reviewed, no clinical detail. | Discuss the patient's details publicly, even to defend himself; argue online; let colleagues post on his behalf |
| Viral negative post or review | Screenshot and log; reply once, briefly, inviting offline contact; report only if it breaches platform rules | Solicit counter-reviews or testimonials (Expl. V bans this outright); threaten |
| Misquote in the press | Message the reporter the same day with the exact correction and the accurate line; escalate to the desk editor only if ignored; post the accurate statement on his own channel | Demand deletion as a first step; go public against the reporter |
| Hospital PR releases something non-compliant in his name | Written objection to corporate communications the same day; request withdrawal | Assume the hospital carries the liability (11.5) |

Preventive habit: record or keep written copies of everything given to media, so a misquote can be shown.

## Risks & things to avoid

1. **Regulatory exposure is now personal and graded** — warning and ethics training for a first violation, up to 6–12 months' suspension for serious ones — and applies even when the hospital or an agency published the material. Highest-risk items: case-based press releases naming him, "top cardiologist" lists, patient testimonials, procedure tallies, bundled screening offers at talks.
2. **Ambiguity in "without promoting their employer organization".** It is unclear whether a TV caption reading "Apollo Hospitals" breaches 8.2(iii). Until the NMC or the state council clarifies, default to name, qualification and specialty, and let the outlet add affiliation if it insists.
3. **Paying for coverage.** Per-mention PR, advertorial "doctor speaks" slots and paid lists are detectable, are the practice the ethics community has criticised for years, and contaminate genuine coverage.
4. **Commenting on celebrities or named patients.** One speculative quote about a public figure's cardiac event can undo years of credibility and breaches confidentiality norms.
5. **Fear-based framing.** "Young people are dropping dead" coverage invites it; clause 8.1(i) bars fear-based marketing and it also drives unnecessary testing. His differentiator should be calm, proportionate advice.
6. **AI-generated text passed off as his.** Medical Dialogues explicitly rejects AI-generated submissions; editors elsewhere increasingly check; the NMC requires AI-origin marking where material. AI can research, structure and translate drafts; the argument and wording must be his.
7. **Over-commitment for an introvert.** A weekly TV habit will burn him out. The plan above caps live appearances and leans on writing and one reusable talk.
8. **Being quoted outside his competence.** Wire copy already presents non-cardiologists as cardiologists; he should decline neurology, diet-fad and supplement questions and refer on.
9. **Dual-hospital confusion.** Two affiliations and two name spellings can produce inconsistent credits; the press kit's "how to credit" line prevents it.
10. **Stale or unverified contacts.** Half the reporter names in circulation could not be confirmed; mis-addressed pitches cost credibility.
