# 32 — "Who may say what": the voice map, and the lawful ways to get there

**Executive summary**

1. **The sponsor's distinction is in the text.** The Guidelines "apply to the professional conduct of the RMPs" (cl. 4.1) and to hospitals. A third party's words become the doctor's only if he "authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted" them (cl. 4.4), or "use[d] or permit[ted]" a patient, employee or third party, or used one "as an intermediary" (cl. 8.1(xi)). A patient, trainee, society or journalist speaking unprompted is outside all of these.
2. **Asking for private feedback is not prohibited by any clause.** The request rule (Explanation V to cl. 3.2, second sentence) is limited to requests "for professional promotion on social media". What is barred in terms is requesting or sharing testimonials or reviews for that purpose, and soliciting "fake, paid or misleading" ones for any promotion.
3. **His own voice is wide for peers and journals, narrower for the public.** Presenting and publishing aggregate, de-identified results is covered by "academic discussions" (5.1), the 8.1(v) exception ("strictly for scientific or educational purposes") and 8.2(i). Stating the same numbers to the lay public is **unsettled**: "achievements" and "reputation" are inside the definition of advertisement (3.2), and the 2002 Regulations say he shall not "boast of cases, operations, cures". The lawful route is: ethics approval → abstract or paper → cite the publication inside public teaching, with denominators and no comparison.
4. **Where the text does not support a relaxed reading:** resharing, quoting or embedding any patient praise (barred in terms); inviting public reviews (unsettled, and barred on a cautious reading, because cl. 3.6 defines "social media" widely enough to take in Google Maps); a hospital or his own staff doing either for him; the helper friend posting praise or patient quotes (the "intermediary" rule); AI-made patient stories (7.2(b)).
5. **Other systems (Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, Alberta, the UK, the US) draw the same line with four tests**: control of the platform, solicitation, selection, and use in advertising. New Zealand's wording is closest to the sponsor's view: a doctor must not encourage testimonials, but "you are not responsible for any unsolicited testimonials or comments" on media "over which you do not have control". The three most valuable ways through are a private patient-experience and outcomes system whose results he publishes as science; a "publish first, then cite" rule for every number; and third-party publication (partner, society, journal, registry) of anything that would be praise if he said it himself.

> **Method note.** Clause text is quoted from `30-factcheck-regulatory-quotations.md` (sections A1–A8, B1–B3), which was checked against the NMC's own PDF. Comparative material was fetched from the regulator's own site wherever it would open. Twelve web searches were used; the rest is direct retrieval. Sites that refused retrieval are named where they matter (GMC, CPSO, Singapore Medical Council, Delhi Medical Council, NICOR, Fortis). This is research, not legal advice.
>
> **Labels.** **[T] Text** = the words say it. **[I] Interpretation** = a reasonable reading, not stated. **[U] Unsettled** = the words do not answer it and no authority has. "Cautious" and "permissive" describe how two careful readers would resolve an [I] or [U] point. Verdicts: **Allowed**, **Amber** (take advice or use the route given), **Barred**.

### 1.1 Explanation V to cl. 3.2 — first sentence

> "No RMP or hospital/medical institution shall solicit, purchase, procure, manipulate or cause to be published fake, paid or misleading patient reviews, ratings, testimonials, endorsements or recommendations for the promotion of medical services."

| Words | Covers | Does not cover | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| "No RMP or hospital/medical institution" | Him, the hospital, and his own clinic (a clinic treating out-patients is within cl. 3.4, per file 11b). | Patients, families, platforms. | [T] |
| "solicit, purchase, procure, manipulate or cause to be published" | Five acts, from asking to arranging. | Receiving, reading, being the subject of. | [T] |
| "fake, paid or misleading" | The three adjectives govern the whole list that follows. | Soliciting a review that is genuine, unpaid and not misleading is **not caught by this sentence**. | [T] on grammar |
| "patient reviews, ratings, testimonials, endorsements or recommendations" | Five nouns, **including ratings**. | Private feedback, complaints, survey answers (not named). | [T] |
| "for the promotion of medical services" | Purpose limb. | Collection for quality improvement. | [T] |
| "misleading" (reach) | Selective asking (only happy patients), edited excerpts, reviews by staff or the doctor's own family, requests to include particular words. Each produces a picture that does not reflect patients generally. | — | [I]. Matches Google's rule against "selectively solicit[ing] positive reviews" and AHPRA's "selectively editing reviews … has the potential to be false, misleading or deceptive". |

- **Cautious reader:** any request that could tilt the result is "misleading"; treat review-gating, timing requests after good outcomes, and staff prompts as barred.
- **Permissive reader:** only deception is caught; a neutral request to every patient is outside this sentence. (It must still pass the second sentence.)

### 1.2 Explanation V — second sentence

> "An RMP shall not request or share patient testimonials, recommendations, endorsements or reviews for professional promotion on social media."

| Words | Covers | Does not cover | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| "An RMP" | The doctor personally. | Hospitals are not named here (they are in the first sentence). His clinic staff are reached through 8.1(xi): "use a third party as an intermediary to do indirectly what the RMP … is prohibited from doing directly". | [T]; staff point [I] |
| "request" | Asking, inviting, prompting, handing a QR code or link, automated messages sent in his name. | Answering a patient's own question; receiving unprompted feedback. | [T] for asking; answering a question is [I] |
| "share" | Reposting, quoting, screenshotting, embedding, reading out, forwarding to a group. | Reading privately; discussing in a clinical audit meeting. A "like" is [U]. | [T] for repost and quote |
| "patient testimonials, recommendations, endorsements or reviews" | Four nouns. | **"Ratings" is absent** from this sentence, though present in the first. "Feedback" and "survey responses" are not named. | [T]. A star rating is a compressed review, so a cautious reader treats it as included. [I] |
| "for professional promotion" | Purpose limb. A request whose answers will be used to promote him. | A request whose answers stay private and are used to improve care. | [T] for the words; purpose is a question of evidence [I] |
| "on social media" | See 1.3. The phrase can attach to "promotion", to "request or share", or to the reviews themselves. On every reading a social-media link is required. | Paper forms, phone calls, a private web form. | [U] as to which word it modifies; [T] that it limits the sentence |

**Three results from the two sentences together**

| Act | Result | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Asking every patient, privately, how the visit went, with answers kept inside the clinic | Not within either sentence: not "fake, paid or misleading", not "for professional promotion", not "on social media". | [T] |
| Asking a patient to record or post a testimonial for Instagram, YouTube or Facebook; reposting a Google review on LinkedIn | Within the second sentence in terms. | [T] |
| A neutral invitation to all patients to leave a review on Google Maps | Depends on (i) whether Google Maps is "social media" under 3.6 and (ii) whether the purpose is "professional promotion". | [U]. Cautious: barred. Permissive: outside, if neutral, universal and unincentivised. |

On (ii): a public review has no use to the doctor's quality system that a private form does not serve better. Its distinctive function is to inform other prospective patients, which is "reputation" within cl. 3.2. A regulator is therefore likely to find the purpose promotional. That is why this report treats the public invitation as amber leaning to barred even on the permissive reading of 3.6. [I]

### 1.3 Cl. 3.6 — what is "social media"?

> "'Social Media' shall mean and include all forms of publications and sharing through internet-based websites, apps, etc. by whatsoever name called, which allows users to create, share and join online communities, participate in social networking, etc."

The definition has three elements: publication or sharing; through an internet site or app; which lets users create, share, join communities or network. It ends with "etc." twice.

| Channel | Within 3.6? | Reasoning | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facebook, Instagram, X, LinkedIn, YouTube | Yes | Core case. | [T] |
| WhatsApp status, broadcast list, group, community, channel | Yes on a cautious reading | Users "create, share and join online communities". Cl. 3.5 lists "messaging platforms (WhatsApp, Telegrams, Facebook etc.)" as a medium of advertisement in any case. | [I] |
| A one-to-one WhatsApp or SMS message to a patient | No | Private correspondence; nothing is published and no community is involved. | [I] |
| Google Maps / Google Business Profile | **Disputed** | *For:* an internet app where users create and share reviews, photos and questions; "by whatsoever name called". *Against:* cl. 3.5 lists "Social media platforms", "websites and blogs", "messaging platforms" and "online healthcare platforms" as separate items, so the drafters treated them as different things; Maps is a search and listing service. | [U] |
| Practo, Justdial, Lybrate and similar | **Disputed**, same arguments | Cl. 3.5 names "online healthcare platforms" separately; cl. 9.4 puts duties on "healthcare platforms hosting RMP". | [U] |
| The clinic's own feedback form (paper, tablet, SMS link to a private form) | No | No publication, no sharing, no community. | [T] |

**Why the label matters less than it seems.** Only the second sentence of Explanation V depends on the words "social media". Every listed channel is in any case a "medium of advertisement" under 3.5 (which "includes but is not limited to" its list). So 6.1, 8.1(ii), 8.1(xi) and 8.1(xii) apply on Google Maps and Practo whatever 3.6 means. [T]

### 1.4 Cl. 3.2 Explanation I — the exemption

> "The discourse of information in the normal course of discharge of information to the public at large, without same being promotional and without any intent for commercial benefit shall be exempted from the above definition of 'Advertisement'."

| Words | Effect | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "in the normal course of discharge of information to the public at large" | Ordinary public information: teaching, answering questions, factual notices. | [T] |
| "without same being promotional" **and** "without any intent for commercial benefit" | Two conditions, both required. The first is about the character of the content; the second is about intent. | [T] |
| Not defined | "promotional". | [T] |

- **Cautious reader:** any mention of his own results, clinic or patients gives the content a promotional character, so the exemption is lost.
- **Permissive reader:** true information given in the ordinary course, with no call to action and no commercial aim, stays exempt even when it cites his own published audit as evidence.

### 1.5 Cl. 4.4 — "authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted"

> "The fact that an advertisement or promotional communication is published through a digital platform, third party, advertising agency, influencer or other intermediary shall not, by itself, absolve an RMP or medical institution from responsibility for content which it has authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted."

| Verb | Ordinary meaning | Examples | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| authorized | Gave permission beforehand. | Approving a hospital advertisement that features him. | [T] |
| commissioned | Asked for it to be made. | Asking the friend, an agency or a patient to produce a post. | [T] |
| sponsored | Paid for or materially supported. | Paid profile, advertorial, "award" with a fee. | [T] |
| **adopted** | Took another's content as his own after the event. | Resharing, quoting, embedding, pinning, featuring on his website. | [T] for reshare, quote, embed. A "like" and a thank-you reply are [U]. |
| **knowingly permitted** | Knew of it, had the power to stop it, and did not. "Permit" presupposes authority to refuse. | A hospital campaign he knows about; testimonials in a review widget on his own website; praise left standing in comments on a page he moderates. | [I] |

**What "knowingly permitted" does not reach** [I]: a review on Google Maps (he cannot remove it; he can only flag policy breaches), a patient's post on the patient's own account, a journalist's article, a trainee's conference slide. He has no power to forbid these, so he does not "permit" them.

**The Indian practice warning.** Councils have read "permit" strictly against doctors where the publisher was their own hospital. In 2020 the Travancore-Cochin Medical Council censured three doctors featured in advertisements that ran in 2019, although one said it "was placed without his knowledge" and another that "his consent was not taken"; the ethics committee's reported view was that doctors "cannot wash their hands of the act of being featured in advertisements" (Medical Dialogues, secondary report). Also in 2020 the council censured 11 doctors for "allowing to be featured" in a hospital newspaper advertisement (reported December 2024). So: with an independent stranger, no power means no permission; with an employer or anyone he has a contract with, silence is treated as permission. [I], Confidence: Medium (news reports, not the orders).

- **Cautious reader:** once he knows of third-party praise and it sits anywhere he has any control, he must act (decline, hide, ask for removal) and keep a record.
- **Permissive reader:** only an affirmative act (asking, approving, reposting) attributes it to him.

### 1.6 Cl. 5.1

|---|---|---|

| "educational content" | Public teaching in any medium. | [T] |

| "not monetized through promotional marketing" | No sponsor-driven promotion. Platform advertising revenue is [U] (file 02). | [U] |

### 1.7 Cl. 6.1 to 6.4 and 7.4 — patient information

| Clause | Operative words | Covers | Does not cover | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | "Patient information, photographs, videos, medical records, clinical images, treatment details, testimonials or other identifiable information shall not be used for advertising purposes except where such use is permitted by applicable law and the necessary consent and other safeguards have been duly complied with." | **Use for advertising** of patient material, including testimonials. | Use for care, audit, research, teaching peers. Aggregate counts are not "identifiable information". | [T] |
| 6.2 | "Patient consent shall not, by itself, make permissible any testimonial, endorsement, promotional patient story, before-and-after depiction, success claim or other advertising practice that is otherwise prohibited" | Closes the "but she consented" argument. | It creates no new prohibition; it refers to practices "otherwise prohibited". | [T] |
| 6.3 | Consent must be "specific, informed, voluntary, documented and capable of verification". | Standard for any consent he relies on, including for surveys and registry use. | — | [T] |

### 1.8 Cl. 8.1(ii)

> "Shall not solicit patients directly or indirectly, promote his/her professional services through self-promotion, engage any third party for marketing medical services, permit the use of their name, image, voice, testimonial or endorsement for promotion of services or any products, participate in promotional campaigns giving an impression of commercialization of medical practice."

| Limb | Reading | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "solicit patients directly or indirectly" | An invitation to review that works as recruitment of the next patient is indirect solicitation. | [I] |
| "engage any third party for marketing medical services" | Agencies, influencers, and a friend if what he does is marketing. Editing educational content is not marketing (file 11). | [I] |
| "permit the use of **their** name, image, voice, testimonial or endorsement for promotion of services or any products" | "Their" refers to the RMP. The testimonial in this limb is **the doctor's own** testimonial or endorsement, given for someone's services or products. It is not about patients' testimonials of him. It also bars letting a hospital, clinic chain or company use his name and face for promotion. | [T] on grammar |
| "permit" | Requires a power to refuse. A journalist or society naming him needs no permission. | [I] |

### 1.9 Cl. 8.1(iii) — does it bar every statement of results?

> "Shall not advertise either personal achievements or professional success rates or number of patients treated with illustrative assertions such as 'Guaranteed cures', 'Best doctors', 'No.1 Specialist', '100% success', 'painless treatment', 'Miracle treatment', 'Exclusive cure' or any other unverifiable or exaggerated claims by whatsoever name called."

| Reading | Grammar | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **A (permissive, and the better grammatical reading)** | "with illustrative assertions such as … or any other unverifiable or exaggerated claims" qualifies "advertise". | What is barred is advertising achievements, success rates or patient numbers **with** such assertions or with unverifiable or exaggerated claims. A verifiable, unexaggerated figure is not caught by this sub-clause. |
| **B (cautious)** | The quoted phrases are only examples of the vice; the mischief is advertising success rates and patient numbers at all. | Any advertising of success rates or numbers treated is barred. Supported by Regulation 6.1.1 of 2002: "nor shall he boast of cases, operations, cures or remedies or permit the publication of report thereof through any mode". |

Status: **[U]**. Two points hold on both readings. The verb is "advertise", so a journal paper or a peer presentation is outside the sub-clause [T]. And a verifiable figure that escapes 8.1(iii) on reading A can still be "self-promotion" under 8.1(ii) or "advertis[ing] surgical results" under 8.1(v) if the setting is promotional [I]. The hospital-profile line "2,300 angiographies, 150 STEMI PCIs, 80 device implants" is "number of patients treated" on a promotional page; on reading B it is barred, and on reading A it must at least be verifiable and dated.

### 1.10 Cl. 8.1(v) and its exception

> "Shall not publish cases for promotional purposes, advertise surgical results, display 'before and after' photographs, publicize celebrity patients, promote personal success stories unless published strictly for scientific or educational purposes with anonymized patient consent."

| Words | Reading | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Five limbs | Cases for promotion; advertising surgical results; before-and-after photographs; celebrity patients; personal success stories. | [T] |
| "unless published strictly for scientific or educational purposes" | An exception. The natural reading is that it applies to the whole list ("published" echoes "publish cases"). "Strictly" excludes mixed purposes. | [I]; a narrow reader attaches it only to the last limb |
| "with anonymized patient consent" | Garbled. Either "anonymised, and with the patient's consent" or "the patient's consent to anonymised use". | [U]. Cautious: do both. |
| Aggregate data | A percentage is not a "case", a photograph or a "personal success story". "Advertise surgical results" needs advertising. | [I] |
| "educational purposes" | Can include public education, not only peer education. | [I]; "strictly" keeps it narrow |

Regulation 7.17 of 2002 adds: he "shall not publish photographs or case reports of his / her patients without their permission, in any medical or other journal in a manner by which their identity could be made out. If the identity is not to be disclosed, the consent is not needed." Cl. 4.2 says the Regulations prevail in an "irreconcilable conflict". The practical answer is to hold a general consent anyway (section 4.4), because it removes the argument.

### 1.11 Cl. 8.1(ix) and its proviso — rankings and awards

> "No RMP or hospital/medical institution shall make comparative claims such as 'best', 'No.1', 'leading', 'most trusted', 'top', 'number one', 'unmatched' or similar superiority claims unless the claim is objectively verifiable, based on a transparent and independently ascertainable methodology and is otherwise permissible under applicable law.
> Provided, Any ranking or award relied upon in advertising shall be capable of independent verification, and material conditions, methodology and relevant date, details of award giving organization or firm shall be disclosed where necessary to avoid misleading the public."

| Point | Reading | Status |
|---|---|---|
| The proviso assumes an award may be "relied upon in advertising". | It sets conditions: independently verifiable; conditions, method, date and awarding body disclosed. | [T] |
| It does not itself permit a doctor to advertise honours. | Regulation 6.1.1 still bars publicity "of such a character as to invite attention to him or to his … achievements, attainments, … honours". Regulation 1.4.2 allows as a suffix only "memberships/honours which confer professional knowledge or recognizes any exemplary qualification/achievements". | [T] |
| Result | Receiving an award is unregulated. Listing it as a dated, sourced line in a CV or factual profile is defensible. Announcing it, celebrating it or using it as a headline is publicity. | [I] |
| Pay-to-receive awards | Not "based on a transparent and independently ascertainable methodology"; create "a misleading impression of professional standing" (8.1(xii)). | [I], strong |

### 1.12 Cl. 8.1(xi) — "use or permit", "prohibited testimonials", "intermediary"

> "No RMP shall use or permit any celebrity, influencer, patient, employee or third party to promote professional services through prohibited testimonials, endorsements or recommendations. … No RMP or hospital/medical institution shall use a third party as an intermediary to do indirectly what the RMP or institution is prohibited from doing directly."

| Words | Reading | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "use or permit" | "Use" needs his agency. "Permit" needs his power to refuse. | [I] |
| "patient, employee or third party" | Patients and his own staff are named. A receptionist handing out review cards is an "employee". | [T] |
| "prohibited testimonials" | The clause does not say which testimonials are prohibited; it points back to Explanation V, 6.1 and 8.1(v). A patient's unprompted post is not "prohibited" by anything, because nothing in the Guidelines binds the patient. | [I] |
| "use a third party as an intermediary" | The anti-avoidance rule. If he may not ask, he may not have the friend, a trainee, the hospital or software ask for him. | [T] |
| What makes someone an "intermediary" | His request, script, incentive, selection or arrangement. A person acting on their own initiative, in their own words, on their own channel is not an intermediary. | [I] |

- **Cautious reader:** anyone inside his system (staff, the helper friend, a paid vendor, a hospital marketing team) is presumed to be his intermediary whenever they speak about him.
- **Permissive reader:** only a proved request or arrangement makes an intermediary.

### 1.13 Cl. 8.1(xii)

> "No RMP or hospital/medical institution shall procure, manipulate or cause any third party to procure or manipulate fake followers, likes, reviews, ratings, testimonials, comments, views or other digital engagement, or manipulate search rankings, visibility or algorithms to create a misleading impression of professional standing."

Covers fake engagement and gaming of rankings [T]. Review-gating and coordinated "please like this" rings are manipulation [I]. Ordinary factual listing hygiene (correct name, address, hours, registration number) is not "manipulat[ing] … to create a misleading impression" [I].

| Sub-clause | Words | Reading | Status |

## 2. The voice map

**How to read it.** Rows are speakers. Columns are acts. For every speaker other than the doctor, the cell answers: *if this speaker does this act about him, what is his position?* The Guidelines bind him, so the verdict is always about his exposure and what he may do. **G** = allowed, **A** = amber, **R** = barred, **–** = does not arise. Two letters (A/R) mean the verdict turns on the condition given in the detail table.

## 3. How other regulators draw this line

### 3.1 Australia (AHPRA and the National Boards) — primary source read

Source: AHPRA, "Testimonials: Understand the requirements" (page reviewed 23/08/2021) and the Advertising Guidelines (in effect 14 December 2020). A 2022 Bill proposed to remove the ban; AHPRA's "National Law changes" page (reviewed 4/06/2025) lists that item under changes removed from the Bill and says "the current ban will remain in place". Confidence: High.

- The rule: "The National Law does not allow the use of testimonials or purported testimonials to advertise regulated health services or a business that provides a regulated health service."
- Definition: testimonials are "recommendations or positive statements about the clinical aspects of a regulated health service used in advertising. Examples include patient stories, patient experiences or success stories."
- Clinical aspects are "Symptom", "Diagnosis or treatment" and "Outcome – the specific outcome or the skills or experience of the practitioner either directly or via comparison."
- Not everything is a testimonial: "Comments that are not about clinical aspects (e.g. comments about customer service or communication style) are not considered testimonials and may be used in advertising."
- **Patients are free:** "The prohibition on using testimonials to advertise regulated health services does not affect: patients sharing information, expressing their views online or posting reviews on review platforms".
- **When the ban applies:** "The prohibition on the use of testimonials only applies when: an advertiser makes use of testimonials (as defined above) to advertise a regulated health service, and/or a person or a business advertises in a way that makes use of the reviews/testimonials to promote the service."
- **The control test:** "An advertiser has control of the advertising if: they publish or authorise content or direct someone to publish or draft content (including a third party, staff member or marketing agency), or there is a mechanism for the advertiser to modify or remove content published by an unrelated publisher."
- **No duty over what he does not control:** "Advertisers do not have to remove or try to remove a review on a website or in social media over which they do not have control."
- **Engaging is risky:** "Advertisers should take care if they choose to engage with reviews on third-party websites as this may be considered using a testimonial to advertise a regulated health service."
- **Own pages:** on a clinic's business social media, compliance "may be achieved by disabling the reviews/testimonials functions".
- **Selection:** "Selectively editing reviews or testimonials has the potential to be false, misleading or deceptive".
- **The three-question tool:** "Is the review about a regulated health service?" → "Is the review used in advertising?" → "Does it refer to a clinical aspect of a regulated health service?" Three yeses: "Not permitted in advertising."
- **Why:** testimonials "are often personal opinions and may have no objective basis"; "the outcomes experienced by one patient do not necessarily reflect the outcome, or likely outcome available to all patients"; "they often include a selection of positive comments".

### 3.2 New Zealand (Medical Council) — primary source read

Source: Medical Council of New Zealand, *Advertising*, April 2022 (next review 2027). Confidence: High.

- Definition of testimonial includes "comments from patients about a doctor's care, skill, treatment, expertise or knowledge", "expressions of appreciation or esteem", "**feedback and endorsements from colleagues**, other health care professionals, friends, family/whānau and other people in the doctor's network", and "comments shared or disseminated from websites, social media platforms or online fora in order to promote your own practice or profile as a doctor".
- Rule: testimonials "must not be used or quoted in your advertising or on any websites, social media fora or any other platforms you control that advertise your services."
- **Solicitation and control, in one footnote:** "You must not encourage patients to leave testimonials on websites or other platforms you control that advertise your or your practice's services, nor should you encourage patients to submit testimonials about your or your practice's services to third party websites. It is your responsibility to monitor regularly the content of such websites or platforms and to remove any testimonials that are posted there. However, you are not responsible for any unsolicited testimonials or comments that are published on a website, in social media or other forms of media over which you do not have control."
- Third parties: "You are responsible for the content of your advertisement, even if a third party wrote the advertisement on your behalf."
- Excluded from "advertising": "material distributed for the purpose of public health or as part of a public health programme".

### 3.3 Singapore (Medical Council) — text read in a reproduction

Source: SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines (2016 edition), Section G, as reproduced verbatim in the Singapore Medical Association advisory of 22 November 2020. The SMC's own PDF and its November 2020 advisory would not open (404 on redirect). Confidence: High for the wording; Medium that no later amendment exists.

- G2(1): information must be "Factual", "Accurate", "Verifiable", "Not misleading", … "**Not laudatory**", "Not comparative", "Not disparaging".
- G2(2): "You may advertise by providing neutrally toned and objective information about your SMC-registered qualifications, experience, areas of practice and your expertise in procedures."
- G2(3): no "'before' and 'after', or even only 'after' images or information … as anecdotal cases create unjustified expectations of the results of treatment".
- **G2(7):** "Testimonials are subjective and must not be used in advertising on any media where you have any control over the content about yourself. You must not ask or induce your patients or anyone to write positive testimonials about you in any media."
- The SMA's covering note: the standard applies "whether a doctor does so directly, or through a proxy that he or she authorises or associates with".
- A November 2020 SMC advisory reportedly warned that joining search-optimisation platforms that use patient ratings may amount to using testimonials (search summary only; not opened). Confidence: Low.

### 3.4 Canada — Alberta read; Ontario not retrievable

**Alberta (CPSA), Advice to the Profession: Advertising (last revised March 2021) — primary source read.** Confidence: High.

- "Third party websites where physicians have no control over the content (e.g., physician rating websites, Google reviews, etc.) are not considered third party advertising. However, if, for example, a physician has a website or social media presence on which patients can provide reviews, recommendations, feedback, opinions, case studies, etc., these would be considered testimonials if they are made public, as physicians have control over whether they are published or kept confidential."
- "While publication by an independent party of all feedback (good and bad) collected in a transparent and fair manner may be acceptable, publication of selected testimonials might not fairly reflect most patients' experiences with the physician's practice. Accordingly, CPSA does not allow testimonials."

**Ontario (CPSO), Advertising policy (approved December 2020) and Ontario Regulation 114/94.** The CPSO pages returned 404 to automated retrieval and e-Laws returned no text. From search summaries only: the regulation bars testimonials in a physician's advertising; CPSO's advice is reported to say that some conduct on third-party sites could amount to a breach, with the example of deleting negative comments while leaving positive ones; a law-firm summary says physicians should not ask patients to post ratings or link to sites that carry testimonials. **Could not verify at source.** Confidence: Low to Medium.

### 3.5 United Kingdom — GMC not retrievable; advertising code read

- **GMC, *Good medical practice* (2024).** The GMC site returned 403. A search extract of the GMC's own page shows paragraph 90 beginning: "When communicating publicly as a medical professional – including using social media, advertising your services, and promoting or endorsing any services or products" the doctor must follow paragraphs 88 and 89. Medical Protection summarises the duty as information that is "accurate, and not false or misleading", "verifiable", and that does "not exploit a lack of medical knowledge or vulnerability". I found **no GMC rule that bans testimonials as such**. Confidence: Medium (secondary).
- **Royal College of Surgeons, *Good Surgical Practice*, domain 4** (fetched through a summarising tool): "Obtain written consent from patients if you are using their photographs and testimonials for promotional purposes"; "Take responsibility for the promotion or advertising carried out by a third party on your behalf." So UK surgeons may use testimonials, with consent. Confidence: Medium.
- **CAP Code and ASA advice — primary source read.** "Marketers must hold documentary evidence to show that a testimonial or endorsement used in a marketing communication is genuine … and must also hold contact details for the person (or organisation) that gives it (rule 3.47)." "Testimonials alone do not constitute substantiation so marketers should not rely on testimonials as support for any direct or implied claims". "Any objective claims made in testimonials, such as those about the efficacy of a product, must be supported by sufficient objective evidence". Rule 12.1: "Objective claims must be backed by evidence, if relevant consisting of trials conducted on people." Rule 12.18: "Marketers must not use health professionals or celebrities to endorse medicines." Confidence: High.
- **Patient feedback as a professional duty.** The RCGP's revalidation guidance states the GMC requirement: "At least once in each revalidation cycle you must reflect on feedback from patients, collected using a formal feedback exercise." It adds: "you should not collect the responses yourself in such a way that patients think you might be able to read them or choose only the best." This is the clearest precedent for the sponsor's point 3: a regulator that *requires* doctors to ask patients for feedback, privately and without selection. Confidence: High for the RCGP text.

### 3.6 United States — AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 9.6.1 — primary source read

- "There are no restrictions on advertising by physicians except those that can be specifically justified to protect the public from deceptive practices."
- "The key issue, however, is whether advertising or publicity, regardless of format or content, is true and not materially misleading."
- "testimonials of patients as to the physician's skill or the quality of the physician's professional services tend to be deceptive when they do not reflect the results that patients with conditions comparable to the testimoniant's condition generally receive."
- "a statement that a physician has cured or successfully treated a large number of cases involving a particular serious ailment is deceptive if it implies a certainty of result and creates unjustified and misleading expectations".
- "These standards require the advertiser to have a reasonable basis for claims before they are used in advertising."

### 3.7 Google's own rules — primary source read

Source: Google Maps user-contributed content policy; Business Profile Help, "Tips to get more reviews". Confidence: High.

- Businesses may "Solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives to do so or attempting to influence the rating or the contents of the review."
- They may not "Offer incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services - in exchange for posting any review or revision or removal of a negative review", nor "Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers".
- "When soliciting reviews, merchants should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, nor should they request that specific content be included."
- Prohibited examples include "Merchants requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews" and requests for "content that identifies a staff member".
- Conflict of interest: "current or former employment, a contractual or consultory relationship, or other professional or personal affiliations … (such as industry competitors, familial relationships, etc.)".
- Google's own advice to businesses: "To leave reviews, you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code." On replies: "Never share a reviewer's private information".

Google therefore permits what the NMC text may forbid. Complying with Google is necessary and not sufficient.

### 3.8 The four tests, and which map onto the NMC wording

| Test | Who uses it | Their words | NMC words it maps to | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Control** of the platform or content | Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, Alberta | "there is a mechanism for the advertiser to modify or remove content"; "platforms you control"; "any media where you have any control over the content about yourself" | 4.4 "knowingly permitted"; 8.1(ii) and 8.1(xi) "permit" | Close. "Permit" implies power to refuse. India has no express sentence freeing the doctor from content he does not control. |
| **Solicitation** | New Zealand, Singapore; Google (incentives and selection only) | "nor should you encourage patients to submit testimonials … to third party websites"; "must not ask or induce your patients or anyone to write positive testimonials about you in any media" | Expl. Singapore's reaches "any media". |
| **Selection or curation** | Australia, Alberta, Google | "Selectively editing reviews"; "publication of selected testimonials"; "selectively solicit positive reviews" | Expl. V "manipulate", "misleading"; 8.1(xii) | Close, by interpretation. |
| **Use in advertising** | Australia, New Zealand, UK, US | "makes use of the reviews/testimonials to promote the service"; "used or quoted in your advertising" | 6.1 "used for advertising purposes"; Expl. | Clinical versus non-clinical content | Australia only | "comments about customer service or communication style … may be used in advertising" | None | No fit. India's words make no such split. |
| Substantiation | UK, US | "Testimonials alone do not constitute substantiation"; "a reasonable basis for claims" | 8.1(iii) "unverifiable"; 8.1(ix) "objectively verifiable" | Close for outcome claims. |

**Reading across.** Every system that bans testimonials still leaves the patient's own voice alone, and fixes the doctor's liability to what he controls, asks for, selects or uses. That is the sponsor's point 1. Two systems (New Zealand and Singapore) go further than the sponsor's point 3 and forbid *encouraging* public testimonials anywhere. India's wording sits between: narrower than Singapore's on its face, but with a "social media" definition wide enough to reach the same result.

## 4. Patient feedback done properly

### 4.1 Two kinds of measure, and what the evidence says

| Kind | What it asks | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **PREM** (patient-reported experience measure) | How the care was delivered: access, listening, explanation, coordination, staff. | NHS Friends and Family Test; CAHPS Clinician & Group Survey; GMC-style patient questionnaire. |
| **PROM** (patient-reported outcome measure) | How the patient is: symptoms, function, quality of life. | Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ-7); Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ-12); EQ-5D-5L; Rose Dyspnea Scale; PHQ-2. |

**Evidence, stated plainly.**
- Measuring patient-reported health status is recommended as part of cardiovascular quality measurement (Rumsfeld et al., AHA scientific statement, *Circulation* 2013;127:2233–49). Confidence: High (citation verified; content from prior knowledge).
- **Collecting a PROM does not by itself improve outcomes.** The PRO-HF trial randomised 1,248 heart-failure clinic patients to KCCQ-12 at every visit, shared with the clinician, or usual care. At one year the adjusted difference in KCCQ-12 was 0.2 points (95% CI −1.7 to 2.0). Its conclusion: "Routine PRO assessment in HF clinic visits did not impact patient-reported quality of life or other clinical outcomes." (Sandhu et al., *Circulation* 2024;149:1717–28.) Confidence: High (abstract read).
- Where symptom reports trigger action, results differ. In oncology, patient-reported symptom monitoring with nurse alerts was associated with longer survival (Basch et al., *JAMA* 2017;318:197–8). Different disease and design; cited as a pointer only. Confidence: High for citation.
- A systematic review of using patient-experience data for improvement exists (Gleeson et al., *BMJ Open* 2016;6:e011907). From prior knowledge, it found that such data mostly led to small process changes and that organisations struggled to act on it. Confidence: Medium (title verified; findings not re-read).

**Meaning for him.** The survey is the instrument. The improvement comes from a monthly review that changes something, followed by a re-measure. This agrees with Addendum 4 ("what works adds measurement, a treatment route, repeated human follow-up").

**ICHOM coronary artery disease set** (McNamara et al., *J Am Heart Assoc* 2015;4:e001767): patient-reported measures are the SAQ-7, the Rose Dyspnea Scale and the PHQ-2, with survival and complications. The paper tracks patient-reported measures "at 30 days, and then annually for 5 years" after an index event, and survival "at 30 days postdischarge, and at 1 and 5 years". **ICHOM heart-failure set** (Burns et al., *JACC Heart Fail* 2020;8:212–22): includes the KCCQ-12; other components from prior knowledge are a physical-function measure and the PHQ-2 (Medium).

**If no Telugu version exists.** Do not translate a licensed instrument informally. The licensor's standard method is two forward translations, reconciliation, back-translation and cognitive interviews with about five patients (ISPOR abstract on KCCQ linguistic validation; search summary). A Telugu SAQ-7 or KCCQ-12 validation would itself be a publishable paper. Until then, use the Telugu EQ-5D-5L, plain clinical measures (LDL, blood pressure, smoking, medicines taken), and the experience questions.

### 4.3 How to collect

| Question | Practice | Source or reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Who asks** | Reception, a nurse or a health educator. Not the treating doctor. | RCGP on GMC revalidation: "you should not collect the responses yourself in such a way that patients think you might be able to read them or choose only the best". |
| **Who is asked** | Every patient in the period, or every patient in one fixed week each month. Never a chosen subset. | Avoids "misleading" (Expl. V) and matches Google's and AHPRA's selection rule. |
| **When** | Experience: at the end of the visit or within a week. Outcomes: at baseline, 30 days, 12 months, then yearly. | NHS guidance allows "asking in real time" or after discharge; ICHOM timeline. |
| **Anonymity** | Experience forms anonymous, into a sealed box "opened by someone else". Outcome forms linked to the record by clinic number, with consent. | RCGP wording; NHS guidance describes the test as "quick and easy, anonymous". |
| **Mode** | Paper in Telugu and English; interviewer-administered by non-treating staff for patients who do not read; phone at 30 days and 12 months. | Interviewer versions of the Telugu EQ-5D exist. |
| **Sensitive cases** | Skip or delay after a death, a serious complication or bad news. | NHS guidance: exclude "patients where it would be insensitive to ask for feedback". |
| **Collation** | Someone other than the doctor enters and totals; ideally an outside person does the annual analysis. | Independence makes the later paper credible. |
| **Free text** | Read for learning. Never published or quoted outside a journal. | 6.1; Expl. V ("share"). The NHS lets providers publish free text only where "the person providing feedback is happy for the feedback to be published", by tick box. India's text is stricter. |
| **Response rate** | Record forms offered and returned. Report both. | STROBE; without the denominator the figure cannot be read. |

Time and cost: design one-off 6–8 hours; printing a few hundred rupees a month; staff 5 minutes per patient for interviewer forms; monthly review 30 minutes. EQ-5D registration and SAQ/KCCQ licences: terms to be confirmed with the owners.

### 4.4 Model consent line (registration form)

> **Use of information to improve care.** This clinic uses information from patient records, with names and all identifying details removed, to check and improve the quality of its care. Combined results (numbers and percentages only) may be presented at medical meetings or published in medical journals. No patient will be identified. You may say no, now or later, and your care will not change.
> ☐ I agree  ☐ I do not agree  Signature / thumb impression ______ Date ______

This line serves three purposes: it meets "specific, informed, voluntary, documented and capable of verification" (6.3); it answers the phrase "with anonymized patient consent" in 8.1(v) on its strictest reading; and it supports an ethics-committee application. It is not a substitute for ethics approval. It must be given in Telugu as well, and re-checked against the DPDP Rules before 13 May 2027 (file 30, E1).

### 4.7 "Where can I leave you a review?" — three answers

| Version | Words | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Cautious** | "Thank you, that means a lot. Doctors are not allowed to ask for or collect public reviews, so I don't. ## 5. His own results in his own voice

### 5.1 The order of disclosure

| Step | What | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Consent line** on the registration form (4.4) and a one-page data dictionary with definitions copied from an established registry. | Lawful basis; comparable definitions. | 6.3; 8.1(v) |
| 2 | **Ethics committee** approval, or a waiver granted by the committee. ICMR 2017 allows a waiver for "retrospective studies, where the participants are de-identified or cannot be contacted"; the committee grants it, the investigator cannot assume it. His own clinic has no committee; Apollo's IEC-BMR states it may review proposals from other sites "until they have their own registered EC" (file 16). | No abstract or journal will take the work without it. | ICMR 2017, section 5.7 and Box 5.2 (file 16) |
| 3 | **Independent check of the denominator.** Someone other than him reconciles the registry against the appointment book, cath-lab log or billing list, and audits a sample of records. | Answers "did he leave out the bad cases?" | STROBE items on participants and bias |
| 4 | **Abstract** to a society meeting (CSI Telangana, CSICON), then a **paper** (Indian Heart Journal or similar). | Peer scrutiny; creates the citable source. | 5.1; 8.2(i) |
| 5 | **Registry submission** where one exists (National Interventional Council of the CSI collects annual centre data). | Puts his numbers inside a third party's method. | NIC 2017 report |
| 6 | **Public citation**, inside teaching, of what is already published. | The only route to column 5 that rests on an express exception. | 8.2(i); 5.1 |

Cautious rule: nothing is said to the public until step 4 has produced a published abstract or paper. Permissive rule: step 2 plus a public data note. This report recommends the cautious rule.

### 5.2 Reporting standards

| Standard | For | Core demands | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **STROBE** | Cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies. | Setting, eligibility, how participants were selected, numbers at each stage, missing data, limitations. The checklist is "not an instrument" for judging quality; it is for reporting. | strobe-statement.org |
| **RECORD** | Studies using "routinely-collected health data (such as health administrative data, electronic medical record data, … and disease registries)". An extension of STROBE. | How records were selected and linked; codes and definitions; data cleaning. | record-statement.org; Benchimol et al., *PLoS Med* 2015;12:e1001885 |
| **SQUIRE 2.0** | Quality-improvement reports: "system level work to improve the quality, safety, and value of healthcare". | Why the work was started, what was changed, how it was studied, what was found, including what did not work. A "SQuIRE 2026" revision is in progress. | squire-statement.org; Ogrinc et al., *BMJ Qual Saf* 2016;25:986–92 |

A clinic audit of 12-month LDL control is a RECORD study. A project that changed the follow-up process and re-measured is a SQUIRE report.

### 5.3 Denominators, small numbers and risk adjustment

- **Always the count and the denominator.** "228 of 371 (61%)", never "61%" alone. State who was eligible, who was lost, and how the lost were treated.
- **Small numbers have wide ranges.** With 150 primary PCIs and 6 deaths, the death rate is 4.0% with a 95% interval of about 1.8% to 8.5% (Wilson method, my calculation). With 1,500 and 60 it is 4.0%, 3.1% to 5.1%. A single operator's event rates are too imprecise for any comparative statement.
- **Case mix decides mortality.** BCIS's public explanation: "An operator who works in a hospital that sees a relatively large number of such patients will have a higher mortality irrespective of how skilled the teams are". Cardiogenic shock carries "about a 50% risk of death". Out-of-hospital arrest cases are listed but excluded from risk-adjusted outcome because "there are no mathematical models yet developed to predict outcomes".
- **How the UK handles it.** Three years of data pooled; survival to 30 days from national death records; a published risk model (McAllister et al., *Int J Cardiol* 2016;210:125–32); a recommended minimum of "75 PCI procedures in a year". Funnel plots are the standard display for comparing units with different volumes (Spiegelhalter, *Stat Med* 2005;24:1185–202).
- **Public reporting has side effects.** In US states with public PCI reporting, patients with acute myocardial infarction were less likely to receive PCI, without a mortality difference (Joynt et al., *JAMA* 2012;308:1460–8; from prior knowledge of the findings, citation verified). Confidence: Medium.
- **Prefer process and intermediate measures.** LDL at goal at 12 months, blood pressure controlled, on statin and antiplatelet at one year, cardiac rehabilitation attended, smoking stopped, door-to-balloon time. They depend less on case mix, need smaller numbers, and are what "After the stent" is about.

## 7. A decision aid

### 7.1 Six questions, in order

1. **Whose voice is it?**
   - Someone else's, on their own channel, and I did nothing to bring it about → **Allowed. Stop. Do not touch it** (no like, reply, reshare, quote or link).
   - Someone else's, but they work for me, act for me, or I can edit or remove it → treat it as **mine** and go on.
   - Mine → go on.
2. **Did I ask for it, script it, reward it, choose it or arrange it?**
   - Yes, and it is praise, a review, a testimonial or a recommendation → **Barred** if it is for a public channel. If the request was for private feedback only → **Allowed**; go to 3 for what I do with the answers.
   - No → go on.
3. **Is it public?**
   - No (clinic team, private form, one-to-one message, peer meeting, journal) → **Allowed**, subject to 4.
   - Yes → go on.
4. **Could any patient be identified, by name, face, voice, date, place or rare detail?**
   - Yes → **Barred** in public (7.4). In a journal: consent and de-identification (Reg. 7.17; 6.4).
   - No → go on.
5. **Is it promotional in purpose or effect?** Would a reasonable reader take it as "choose this doctor"? Does it name the clinic, invite a booking, compare, or celebrate?
   - Yes → **Barred**, or strip it back to teaching.
   - No, it teaches or reports → go on.
   - Not sure → **Ask the lawyer**.
6. **Is it verifiable?** Is there a published source, a denominator, a method and a date?
   - Yes → **Allowed**. Cite the source.
   - Not yet → **Wait.** Publish first (8.2(i)).

**Three overrides.** Anything on the Barred list in 7.2 stays barred whatever the answers. Anything involving a hospital's marketing team gets a written "no" unless it is purely educational. Anything new that the six questions do not settle goes to the lawyer before it is done, not after.

### 7.2 Always barred, whatever the route

Reposting, quoting or embedding patient praise. Asking for reviews on Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn or X. Rewards, discounts or gifts for any review. Asking only happy patients. Reviews by staff, family or the friend. Star ratings or testimonial widgets on anything he controls. Naming or thanking a patient in public. Supplying a patient to a journalist or a hospital campaign. "Best", "top", "No.1", "most trusted". Paid awards, paid rankings, paid profiles. AI-written patient stories, reviews or endorsements; an AI avatar or cloned voice of him.

## Risks & things to avoid

1. **Treating Google's permission as the rule.** Google invites businesses to ask for reviews by link or QR code. The NMC text may forbid it. Follow the stricter.
2. **The slow slide from private to public.** A private form that ends "…and if you were happy, please also review us on Google" is a selective public request. Keep the two apart; say on the form that public reviews are not sought.
3. **Selection at any stage.** Asking only some patients, analysing only some records, or quoting only some comments turns honest material into "misleading" material (Explanation V; 8.1(xii)).
4. **A "like" as a habit.** It is the smallest act of adoption and the easiest to screenshot in a complaint. The Telangana council's complaint form is open to anyone, including competitors (file 02).
5. **Replying warmly to praise.** It confirms a patient relationship and joins his voice to the testimonial. AHPRA warns that engaging with reviews "may be considered using a testimonial to advertise".
6. **Results as headlines.** A true figure in a promotional setting is still an advertisement of "achievements". Keep numbers inside teaching and inside citations.
7. **Small-number claims.** With 150 cases, a death rate has a range several points wide. Never compare.
8. **A patient group that he runs.** It becomes a controlled platform; what members say there is "knowingly permitted".
9. **The friend's reach.** A well-followed friend praising him is an "influencer-led" communication within cl. 3.2. Unprompted or not, it will be read as arranged.
10. **Hospital marketing.** The hospital carries little risk before a medical council; he carries all of it. Silence is read as consent.
11. **Paid recognition.** Any award, list or profile with a fee fails 8.1(ix) and 8.1(xii) and taints the genuine ones.
12. **Informal translation of licensed questionnaires.** It breaches the licence and produces data no journal will accept.
13. **Asking the Council a question too early.** A written negative answer closes the permissive reading for him. Lawyer first.
14. **Relying on this file as legal advice.** The amber cells are amber because no authority has spoken.
