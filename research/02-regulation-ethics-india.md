# The legal, regulatory and ethical boundary for a doctor doing PR, publicity and social media in India (as of 12 November 2026)

**Executive summary**

1. **The rules changed five weeks ago.** On 6 October 2026 the NMC issued "Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication by Hospitals/Medical Institutions and RMPs" (No. R-13014/01/2024-Ethics), effective immediately. I read the 13-page primary text. It defines "advertisement" to include any communication intended to promote a doctor's *reputation*, skills or achievements, so a "PR system" is inside the regulated zone by definition.
2. **What is allowed is public education, not promotion.** Health-awareness content, lectures in his own name, journal publications, formal announcements and a factual hospital directory listing are expressly permitted. Education must not promote his practice, solicit patients or be "monetized through promotional marketing".
3. **Patient success stories and testimonials are prohibited even with consent** (clause 6.2), as are before/after images, "best/top/No.1" claims, requesting or sharing reviews for promotion, bought followers, paid rankings, product endorsements and AI-generated promotional campaigns. Penalties run from a warning to removal from the register for 1–3 years, imposed by the Telangana Medical Council (TGMC).
4. **The binding regulation is still the 2002 Ethics Regulations.** The NMC's 2023 Professional Conduct Regulations remain "held in abeyance" (since 23 August 2023) and have no legal force, but most of their social-media guidance has now been re-issued through the 2026 Guidelines.
5. **Practical consequence:** build the programme as a *public health education and academic contribution programme* that the doctor personally approves, with registration number on every post, zero patient-identifiable content, no review solicitation, no paid promotion and no endorsements. Reputation is then a by-product, which is the only version of this project that is safe.

> **Method note.** The shared web-search budget ran out after the first four searches, so most of this report rests on direct retrieval of primary documents from regulator websites (NMC, TGMC, MeitY, Department of Pharmaceuticals, Google, AMA) rather than on search. Where I could not retrieve a primary source I say so and mark the claim Medium or Low. This is research, not legal advice; a healthcare lawyer should confirm before launch.

### 1. Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002

Source: official NMC PDF, "amended upto 8th October 2016" (https://www.nmc.org.in/storage/cms/Ethics-Regulations-2002.pdf). Confidence: High.

**What is prohibited**

- **6.1.1 Soliciting and self-publicity.** "Soliciting of patients directly or indirectly, by a physician, by a group of physicians or by institutions or organisations is unethical." A physician shall not make use of himself or his name "as subject of any form or manner of advertising or publicity through any mode... which is of such a character as to invite attention to him or to his professional position, skill, qualification, achievements, attainments, specialities, appointments, associations, affiliations or honours and/or of such character as would ordinarily result in his self aggrandizement."
- **6.1.1 Endorsements and boasting.** He shall not give "any approval, recommendation, endorsement, certificate, report or statement with respect of any drug, medicine, nostrum remedy, surgical, or therapeutic article, apparatus or appliance or any commercial product", "nor shall he boast of cases, operations, cures or remedies or permit the publication of report thereof through any mode."
- **6.1.2 Photographs.** "Printing of self photograph, or any such material of publicity in the letter head or on the sign board of the consulting room... shall be regarded as acts of self advertisement and unethical conduct". Sketches and diagrams of the human system are allowed.
- **6.4 Rebates and commissions.** No gift, commission or fee-splitting for referring patients.
- **7.11 Press.** A physician "should not contribute to the lay press articles and give interviews regarding diseases and treatments which may have the effect of advertising himself or soliciting practices".
- **7.13 Signboards.** Improper to use "an unusually large sign board" or to write anything other than name, university/statutory qualifications, titles, speciality and registration number with the State Medical Council. Same content for prescription paper.
- **7.19** No touts or agents for procuring patients. **7.20** No claim to be a specialist without a special qualification in that branch.
- **2.2, 7.14 Confidentiality.** Patient confidences are not to be revealed except under court order, serious identified risk, or notifiable disease.
- **7.17 Case images.** He "shall not publish photographs or case reports of his/her patients without their permission, in any medical or other journal in a manner by which their identity could be made out. If the identity is not to be disclosed, the consent is not needed."

**What is expressly permitted**

- **Formal announcements in the press** (6.1.1): on starting practice; change of type of practice; changing address; temporary absence from duty; resumption of another practice; succeeding to another practice; public declaration of charges.
- **Public health writing and speaking in his own name** (7.11): he "is open to write to the lay press under his own name on matters of public health, hygienic living or to deliver public lectures, give talks on the radio/TV/internet chat for the same purpose and send announcement of the same to lay press." Note that "internet chat" is in the original text; online education has been permitted since 2002.
- **Institutional advertising** (7.12): a nursing home or private hospital run by a physician may be advertised, limited to name, type of patients admitted, training and facilities offered, and fees.
- **Registration number display** (1.4.1) is mandatory in the clinic and on prescriptions, certificates and receipts. **Suffixes** (1.4.2) are limited to recognised degrees and memberships/honours that "confer professional knowledge or recognise any exemplary qualification/achievements".

**Punishment** (8.2): the State Medical Council holds an enquiry, hears the doctor, and "may award such punishment as deemed necessary or may direct the removal altogether or for a specified period, from the register". Deletion "shall be widely publicised in the local press".

### 2. NMC RMP (Professional Conduct) Regulations, 2023 and "Guidelines on conduct of RMPs on social media"

Source: Gazette text reproduced in the NMC's own e-Compendium 2020–2023, "Regulations" volume (https://www.nmc.org.in/publications/e-compendium-2020-2023). Confidence: High on text.

**2.1 What they said**

- **Reg. 11 Advertisement.** (A) Formal announcement "in any media (print, electronic or social) within 3 months" on the same seven events as 2002. (B) An RMP "or any other person including corporate hospitals" may place announcements containing nothing more than name of institution, type of patients, kind of doctors and staff training, facilities and fees. This was the first attempt to put hospitals under the same rule. (C) "RMP is allowed to do public education through media without soliciting patients for himself or the institution."
- **Reg. 10** repeated the endorsement ban, extended to "software/platforms". **Reg. 9** added "RMP shall not use online forums or agents for procuring patients."
- **Reg. 35** barred gifts, travel, hospitality, consultancy fees or honoraria from pharma, device companies and corporate hospitals, and barred involvement in third-party CPD with pharma sponsorship.
- **Guideline-6, Conduct of RMPs on Social Media** (the gazette numbers it Guideline-6; it is referenced from Reg. 20). Its eleven points, in substance:
  1. Information posted must be factual and verifiable, not misleading, and must not "exploit the patient's vulnerability or lack of knowledge".
  2. Avoid discussing patients' treatment or prescribing on public social media; if approached, "guide the patient toward a telemedicine consultation or in-person consultation".
  3. "RMPs should not post patients' photographs or scan images (ct/pet scans) on social media."
  4. Conduct towards colleagues follows general medical ethics.
  5. No "purchasing 'likes', 'followers', or paying money so that search algorithms lead to their name being listed at the top or registering on software programs (apps) that charge fees for higher ratings or soliciting patients."
  6. "RMPs should not request or share patients' testimonials or recommendations or endorsements or reviews in social media."
  7. Refrain from "sharing images of healed/cured patients, or surgery/procedure videos or images displaying impressive results under any circumstances."
  8. Educative material is allowed, but "communication should be limited to the expertise of the RMP."
  9. The RMP's webpage follows the same rules.
  10. No boundary crossings; dignity and decorum.
  11. "Soliciting of patients directly or indirectly through social media is unethical."
- A separate consent guideline added: "Under no circumstances will the patient's data be posted on social media."
- Penalty levels ran from L1 (advisory/warning/ethics training) through L2 (suspension up to 30 days), L3 (up to 3 months), L4 (3 months to 3 years) to L5 (permanent). Social-media breaches were tagged L1.

**2.2 The abeyance**

The 2023 Regulations were gazetted on 2 August 2023. On 23 August 2023 the NMC notified the "Registered Medical Practitioners (Professional Conduct) (Amendment) Regulations, 2023" (No. R-12013/01/2022/Ethics), which state that the 2023 Regulations "are hereby held in abeyance with immediate effect", "shall not be operative and effective till further Gazette Notification", and that the Commission "adopts and makes effective with immediate effect" the 2002 Regulations "as if the same have been made by the Commission". Reporting attributes the reversal to IMA and industry objections, mainly to mandatory generic prescribing (Medical Dialogues, Aug 2023). Confidence: High.

**2.3 Current status in 2026: precisely**

- The 2023 Regulations are **in abeyance, not repealed and not in force**. Press sometimes says "withdrawn" (ThePrint, 1 Apr 2024); the legal instrument says "held in abeyance".
- I found **no Gazette notification reviving them**. Three pieces of evidence that the 2002 Regulations are still the operative code: (a) the NMC's own 6 October 2026 notice is framed under "provisions of Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002"; (b) a TGMC final order dated 10 March 2026 punishes under "Regulation 8.2 of the Indian Medical Council... Regulations, 2002"; (c) the NMC "What's New" list through 6 November 2026 shows no revival or replacement. Confidence: Medium-High (I could not run a gazette search).
- Guideline-6 therefore has **no independent legal force**. But its key prohibitions (testimonials, bought likes/followers, paid search ranking, paid app ratings, soliciting via social media) have been **re-issued almost word for word in the October 2026 Guidelines**, which do apply. The parts not carried over (no scan images at all, no procedure videos "under any circumstances", stay within your expertise, redirect patients to a proper consultation) should be treated as best practice.

### 2A. The NMC Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication, 6 October 2026 (the document that now governs this project)

Source: NMC public notice No. R-13014/01/2024-Ethics, digitally signed by Dr Raghav Langer, Secretary NMC, 06-10-2026; listed on NMC "What's New" (https://nmc.org.in/whats-new/download/1874). I read all 13 pages. Confidence: High.

**Origin and legal character.** Framed "in view of the Writ Petition (Civil) No.1160/2023 filed in the Hon'ble Supreme Court" (Dr Aniruddha Malpani's PIL on corporate-hospital advertising; notice was issued 10 Oct 2023 per LiveLaw/Cyril Amarchand) and the 2002 Regulations. They are guidelines under a public notice. They state they operate "without altering, overriding or derogating from any existing statutory or regulatory requirement" (2.1 f), are to "be read with" the 2002 Regulations, and "in case of any irreconcilable conflict, the applicable statutory provisions and operative regulations shall prevail" (4.2). So they are best understood as the regulator's authoritative interpretation of clause 6.1 for the digital era, enforced through the existing misconduct machinery. Whether every element (for example the monetary penalty) could survive a court challenge is untested; nobody should volunteer to be the test case.

**Definitions that matter**

- **"Advertisement"** (3.2): "any form of communication, promotion, publicity, announcement, representation or dissemination of information in any medium, which directly or indirectly solicits, promotes or is intended to promote the professional services, **reputation**, skills, qualifications, achievements, facilities or commercial healthcare services of an RMP". Digital, sponsored, targeted, influencer-led, search-engine, platform-based and AI-generated communications count "where their content or manner of dissemination has a promotional character."
- **Explanation I (the safe harbour):** "The discourse of information in the normal course of discharge of information to the public at large, without same being promotional and without any intent for commercial benefit shall be exempted".
- **Explanation III (new duty):** "RMP while publishing any Electronic media posts should transparently disclose their name, qualifications, registration status and SMR/NMR Registration number."
- **Explanation V:** No RMP or hospital "shall solicit, purchase, procure, manipulate or cause to be published fake, paid or misleading patient reviews, ratings, testimonials, endorsements or recommendations". And: "An RMP shall not request or share patient testimonials, recommendations, endorsements or reviews for professional promotion on social media."
- **"Medium of advertisement"** (3.5) includes print, TV, radio, social media, websites and blogs, messaging platforms (WhatsApp, Telegram), podcasts, influencer marketing, sponsored posts, AI-generated promotion, "public interviews, news articles intended for promotion", and online healthcare platforms.
- **Responsibility** (4.4): publication through "a digital platform, third party, advertising agency, influencer or other intermediary shall not, by itself, absolve an RMP" for content he "authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted."

**Permitted**

- 5.1: "Medical Practitioners may participate in health awareness programmers, public health campaigns, academic discussions, educational content, provided that such activities do not promote personal practice, do not solicit patients and are not monetized through promotional marketing."
- 8.2(i): "Only research-based communication, wherein they have published interesting case studies in reputed medical journals shall be allowed."
- 8.2(ii): the seven formal announcements. 9.3 adds they must be "factual, non-promotional and limited in scope".
- 8.2(iii): "lectures or talks on public health and diseases for public awareness through television, radio and electronic media in their own name and designation, **without promoting their employer organization**."
- 8.2(iv): health-education pamphlets for patients visiting the clinic, without guarantees, superiority or unsubstantiated claims.
- 8.2(v): greetings on national or profession-related days, without "advertisement of a professional gains".
- 9.1: a hospital website doctor directory with "names, recognised qualifications, specialties, registration details and availability" is "materially distinct from a paid or promotional advertisement".
- 7.3 Explanation: campaigns on central or state government programmes "in larger public interest" are exempt.

**Prohibited for the doctor (8.1)**

- (i) Creating unnecessary demand; fear-based marketing.
- (ii) Soliciting directly or indirectly; "promote his/her professional services through self-promotion, engage any third party for marketing medical services, permit the use of their name, image, voice, testimonial or endorsement for promotion of services or any products".
- (iii) Advertising "personal achievements or professional success rates or number of patients treated with illustrative assertions such as 'Guaranteed cures', 'Best doctors', 'No.1 Specialist', '100% success', 'painless treatment'... or any other unverifiable or exaggerated claims".
- (iv) Any endorsement of a drug, device, diagnostic, "health product or commercial product", "whether or not monetary or other consideration is received."
- (v) "Shall not publish cases for promotional purposes, advertise surgical results, display 'before and after' photographs, publicize celebrity patients, promote personal success stories unless published strictly for scientific or educational purposes with anonymized patient consent."
- (vi) Statements that create unrealistic expectations, conceal risks or promote unproven therapies.
- (vii)–(viii) No commission, referral or lead-generation fees; agency, influencer or platform deals must not link payment "to the procurement or referral of individual patients."
- (ix) No "best", "No.1", "leading", "most trusted", "top" unless objectively verifiable by transparent methodology; any award relied on must be independently verifiable with methodology, date and issuer disclosed.
- (x) No discounts, limited-period offers, coupons, cashbacks, "free procedures or similar inducements" likely to encourage unnecessary care.
- (xi) No use of "any celebrity, influencer, patient, employee or third party to promote professional services through prohibited testimonials, endorsements or recommendations"; no doing "indirectly what the RMP... is prohibited from doing directly."
- (xii) No procuring or manipulating "fake followers, likes, reviews, ratings, testimonials, comments, views" or manipulating "search rankings, visibility or algorithms to create a misleading impression of professional standing."

**Patient privacy (6, 7.4, 7.6)**

- 6.1: patient information, photos, videos, records, images and testimonials shall not be used for advertising except where law permits and consent and safeguards are in place.
- **6.2: "Patient consent shall not, by itself, make permissible any testimonial, endorsement, promotional patient story, before-and-after depiction, success claim or other advertising practice that is otherwise prohibited".**
- 6.3: where consent is required it must be "specific, informed, voluntary, documented and capable of verification".
- 6.4: even where disclosure is lawful, names, birthmarks and identifying marks must be cropped, blurred or blacked out.
- 7.4: no sharing of "patient data, such as names, faces, anomalies, etc., which can be identified publicly."
- 7.6: digital personal data use must comply with the IT Act, 2000 and the DPDP Act, 2023 and 2025 Rules.

**AI (7.2).** "AI Generated promotional campaign for furtherance of commercial interest is prohibited." Compliant AI-generated content "shall compulsorily carry the source mark, explicitly stating that the origin of the content is AI." AI must not be used to create misleading claims, to manipulate "a patient's image, testimonial, voice or clinical outcome", or to create a synthetic endorsement "purporting to be that of an actual patient, RMP or other person." Patient information used as AI input must comply with privacy law and professional ethics.

**Hospitals (8.3, 9).** May publish factual institutional information only. Their communication must not contain solicitation, inducement, superiority claims, guarantees, "or promotional portrayal of an individual RMP." 9.2: use of an RMP's photograph is not permitted "where such use constitutes self-advertisement, solicitation or promotional publicity." 9.4: healthcare platforms hosting RMPs must prohibit "paid ranking of doctors".

**Penalties and procedure (10, 11).** Graded action that "may be contemplated" by the State Medical Council: first violation, warning and mandatory ethics training; second, censure and monetary penalty; third, suspension 3–6 months; serious violation ("Misleading cure claims, inducement for patients, digital mass solicitation, etc."), suspension 6–12 months; repeated violations, removal for 1–3 years. Show-cause notice and reasoned order are required. Appeal to EMRB/NMC within 60 days (s.30(3)); second appeal within 60 days (s.30(4)). Hospitals are dealt with under the Clinical Establishments Act or state law, but where an RMP "has personally authorized, participated in, endorsed or otherwise been responsible for the prohibited communication", his conduct "may separately be examined" (11.5).

**Post-issue developments.** Coverage by Medical Dialogues (7 Oct), The South First (7 Oct) and Telangana Today (8 and 9 Oct 2026) reports the text only; none carries a reaction from IMA or TGMC. No NMC clarification appears on its "What's New" list through 6 November 2026. Compliance blogs written by marketing firms flag two open ambiguities: whether asking every patient for an honest Google review is "requesting reviews for professional promotion on social media", and what "monetized through promotional marketing" covers. Confidence: Medium (no search available to look for IMA statements or court challenges).

### 3. Telangana Medical Council and enforcement elsewhere (2023–2026)

**Telangana (primary documents from https://onlinetsmc.in/downloads/). Confidence: High.**

- **What TGMC actually enforces.** Its published actions are about quackery, unrecognised qualifications and registration lapses, not YouTube content. I found **no published TGMC order against a registered doctor for social-media advertising, YouTube content or a "best doctor" claim. Could not verify that any exists.**
- **26 Sept 2025 press release:** five doctors suspended under the 2002 Regulations and the Telangana Medical Practitioners Registration Act, 1968. Three hospital medical directors got 6 months for sponsoring or associating with a quacks' event; one Jubilee Hills clinic doctor got 1 year "for misrepresenting himself as a qualified cosmetologist/hair transplant surgeon without recognized qualifications"; one doctor got 6 months "for displaying unrecognized medical qualifications not approved by NMC."
- **10 March 2026 final order** (suo motu, Apple Scan Centre, Suryapet): a doctor was removed from the register for two years for displaying "MD (Radio Diagnosis)" and "Consultant Radiologist" on the name board, signage and reports without a recognised qualification (Regulation 7.20), with an expired registration. Appeal lies to NMC within 60 days.
- **28 July 2025 public notice:** anyone "advertising and displaying" unrecognised diploma or fellowship certifications "will face strict legal action".
- **10 April 2024 circular:** inspections found most GHMC-area hospitals "not displaying the registration numbers issued by the TSMC against the names of consultant doctors"; consultants practising without Telangana registration or renewal were flagged.
- **3 Jan 2025:** show-cause notice to Rushcare Hospital, Hyderabad over a WhatsApp advertisement tied to an unqualified practitioners' meet; TGMC vice-chairman Dr G. Srinivas said hospitals using agents for marketing would face action (Medical Dialogues).
- **Dec 2024:** TGMC identified 35 people posing as doctors on social media with 1–3 lakh followers (Medical Dialogues).
- **Oct 2026:** TGMC published a formal "Complaint-cum-Declaration" format for complaints against doctors. Anyone, including a competitor, can file.
- Telangana Today (9 Oct 2026) frames the NMC guidelines as aimed at "doctors and hospitals in Hyderabad and across Telangana that use aggressive digital marketing".

**Other states (Confidence: Medium-High, via Medical Dialogues reports)**

- **Kerala, Dec 2024:** State Medical Council warned that hospital advertisements carrying doctors' photographs and qualifications violate clause 6.1. In 2020 it had **censured 11 doctors** over an Aster MIMS Kozhikode advertisement. This is the clearest precedent that a doctor is personally liable for appearing in his hospital's ad.
- **Maharashtra, Dec 2024:** MMC administrator Dr Vinky Rughwani: "Advertisement by doctors is not allowed, even on social media." Seven complaints were pending, some over "100% guaranteed treatment". Sharing clinic details, hours and location was said to be acceptable; third-party promotion and success-story testimonials were not.
- **Karnataka, July 2024:** show-cause notice to an OBGYN with over a million Instagram followers for a reel recommending skin-lightening products, on a complaint from the state dermatologists' association. Lesson: complaints come from peers, and product recommendations outside one's speciality are the trigger.
- **Tamil Nadu, June 2019 (older, possibly stale):** notices to about 100 practitioners for listings on advertising websites and apps, under 6.1.1.

### 4. Patanjali litigation, DMR Act, Consumer Protection Act, CCPA, ASCI

**Patanjali / IMA v. Union of India.** IMA sued in August 2022 over Patanjali advertisements attacking modern medicine. The Supreme Court restrained the ads in February 2024, initiated contempt, closed contempt in August 2024 after apologies, and on 14 August 2025 dismissed the IMA's plea and upheld the removal of a rule on AYUSH advertisements (Wikipedia summary citing The Hindu, Indian Express, Times of India). Confidence: Medium (secondary).

Fallout relevant to doctors, from my prior knowledge and not re-verified this session (Confidence: Medium):
- The Court's 7 May 2024 order held that endorsers, including celebrities and influencers, share responsibility for misleading advertisements, and required advertisers to file self-declarations before ads run; the I&B Ministry later narrowed the self-declaration to food and health sector advertisers.
- The bench also turned on the profession, telling the IMA to put its own house in order over unethical practices by doctors, and the IMA president issued an apology after criticising the Court in a press interview.
- Net effect: the judiciary has signalled that health claims and medical publicity are an enforcement priority, and the October 2026 NMC Guidelines are themselves a product of Supreme Court pressure.

**Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954.** Prohibits advertisements of drugs claiming to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat or prevent scheduled conditions. The Schedule's 54 entries include **heart diseases, high/low blood pressure, diabetes, obesity and arteriosclerosis**. First conviction up to 6 months' imprisonment; subsequent up to 1 year (Wikipedia). For a cardiologist this means: never lend name, face or quote to any product advertised for the heart, BP, sugar or weight. Bona fide scientific books and treatises are exempt under s.14 (from memory; Medium). Confidence: Medium-High.

**Consumer Protection Act, 2019.** Misleading advertisement is an offence; the CCPA can order discontinuation, "impose penalties up to Rs 10 lakhs", and bar an endorser from endorsing for up to one year, three years on repeat (Wikipedia). Subsequent-contravention penalty up to Rs 50 lakh (from memory; High). The CCPA's 2022 Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements require endorser due diligence and disclosure of material connections (from memory; Medium-High).

**CCPA "Additional Influencer Guidelines for Health and Wellness Celebrities, Influencers and Virtual Influencers" (August 2023).** From prior knowledge, not re-fetched (official site refused the connection): certified medical practitioners and health experts must disclose that they are certified when sharing health information or making health claims; others presenting as health experts must carry a disclaimer that content is not a substitute for professional medical advice; general wellness advice not tied to a product or a specific condition is outside scope. Confidence: Medium. This lines up with NMC Explanation III (registration disclosure on posts).

**ASCI Code** (https://www.ascionline.in/the-asci-code/). Claims "should be capable of substantiation"; ads addressed to people with illness require "special care and restraint"; ads must contain "nothing which is in breach of the law". ASCI has no power over doctors but refers health-sector ads to regulators; Cyril Amarchand (Oct 2024) cites ASCI reporting 190 misleading advertisements by clinics and hospitals. ASCI's homepage also links September 2026 "Guidelines for Responsible Labelling of Synthetically Generated Content in Advertising" (title seen, text not read). ASCI's health-influencer qualification rules could not be retrieved. Confidence: Medium.

### 5. Patient privacy and consent

**DPDP Act, 2023** (primary text, MeitY).
- s.6(1): consent must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action" and limited to data "necessary for such specified purpose". The Act's own illustration uses a telemedicine app.
- s.5: a notice must precede consent, stating the data, the purpose, how to withdraw and how to complain. Notice must be available in English or any Eighth Schedule language (Telugu qualifies).
- s.3(c)(ii): the Act does not apply to personal data "made or caused to be made publicly available by the Data Principal". A patient who posts their own story has not thereby licensed the doctor to reuse it for promotion; NMC clause 6.2 still applies.
- s.7(f): processing without consent is allowed for a medical emergency. That covers treatment, not publicity.
- Schedule penalties: up to Rs 250 crore for failure of security safeguards; up to Rs 200 crore for failing to notify a breach; up to Rs 50 crore for other breaches.

**Commencement.** Institutional provisions took effect 13 November 2025; Rules were notified 13/14 November 2025 (sources differ by a day); consent-manager provisions on 13 November 2026; **all substantive obligations (notice, consent, fiduciary duties, rights, penalties) on 13 May 2027** (AZB & Partners; SCC Online; Wikipedia). Rule 3 requires a standalone plain-language notice with itemised data and purpose and a withdrawal link. Withdrawal triggers erasure. Confidence: High.

**Until May 2027** the IT Act's SPDI Rules, 2011 (health information as sensitive personal data requiring consent; from memory, High) and the confidentiality clauses of the 2002 Regulations govern. In practice the **hospital is the data fiduciary** for records, angiograms and echo loops held in its systems. A consultant exporting images from hospital PACS to a personal phone for social media is a hospital-policy and data-protection problem even if the image is anonymised.

**Case images and de-identification.** Clause 7.17 (2002) allows publication without consent only if identity cannot be made out. The 2026 Guidelines require "anonymized patient consent" even for scientific or educational case publication (8.1 v), and cropping or blurring of identifiers (6.4). The ESC's social-media review (EHJ Digital Health 2020) warns that combinations such as rare profession, age and location can identify a patient, and that "Once information is made public, it is impossible to correct or withdraw." DICOM overlays, dates, hospital names, accession numbers, voices in cath-lab video, tattoos and rare anatomy are all identifiers.

**Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020.** Misconduct examples include using patient images and data without consent and: RMPs "are not permitted to solicit patients for telemedicine through advertisements or inducement" (wording confirmed from the version reproduced in the NMC compendium; the 2020 MoHFW PDF returned HTTP 403). Confidence: Medium-High.

**Hospital-employer policy (Apollo, Citizens).** I could not find or verify a public Apollo doctor media or social-media policy. Could not verify. Large hospital groups normally route media through corporate communications and restrict use of the brand; his consultant agreement will say. Note the two-way squeeze in the 2026 Guidelines: he may speak "without promoting [his] employer organization", and the hospital may not run "promotional portrayal of an individual RMP".

### 6. Google reviews, testimonials, and hospital versus doctor advertising

**Google's own rules** (Maps user-contributed content policy; Business Profile help). Confidence: High.
- "Fake engagement is not allowed and will be removed." Reviews must not be "paid for, directly or in kind".
- Businesses must not "offer incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" for reviews, or for changing or removing a negative one.
- Businesses must not "discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers."
- Conflict of interest: reviews by current or former employees, or family, are not allowed.
- Do not post "medical information" about others without consent. Google's reply guidance: do not share private information; keep replies short and non-promotional.
- Google itself encourages asking all customers for reviews via a link or QR code.

**NMC stance.** Stricter than Google. Explanation V bars fake, paid or misleading reviews outright, and says an RMP "shall not request or share" patient reviews "for professional promotion on social media". The definition of social media (3.6) is wide enough to arguably include Google Maps. Compliance commentators admit they cannot tell whether a neutral, non-selective request to every patient is caught (MapMyClinic, 9 Oct 2026).

**Working position**
- Unprompted reviews left by patients: fine. He did nothing.
- The doctor personally asking patients for reviews, review QR cards on his desk with his name, follow-up WhatsApp asking for five stars: treat as prohibited.
- Screenshotting, quoting, embedding or reposting reviews on his website or social profiles: prohibited ("share").
- Incentives, review-gating, staff or family reviews: prohibited by both Google and NMC.
- Hospital-run, all-patient, neutral feedback collection: the hospital's call under its own legal advice; he should not drive it.
- Replying to reviews: permitted by Google; never confirm that the reviewer is a patient, never mention clinical detail, keep it generic.

**Hospitals versus individual doctors.** Before October 2026 hospitals sat in a regulatory gap: the 2002 Regulations bind doctors, and clinical-establishment laws rarely regulate advertising (Cyril Amarchand, Oct 2024). The 2026 Guidelines close the gap in principle by applying the same content limits to hospitals, with penalties under the Clinical Establishments Act or state law. I could not verify what Telangana's clinical-establishment law says on advertising. What matters for Dr. Avinash is unchanged by the gap: **when a hospital advertises him, he is the one on the medical register.** Kerala censured doctors, not the hospital.

Corporate entities can lawfully do: factual facility and service information, accreditation, fees, emergency numbers, a doctor directory, and government-programme campaigns. They cannot do: "Hyderabad's best cardiologist" creatives, doctor-centred reels, patient testimonial videos naming the doctor, package discounts built around him.

### 7. Pharma and device industry relationships

**His own duty: clause 6.8 of the 2002 Regulations** (primary). Confidence: High.
- No gifts; no travel "for attending conferences, seminars, workshops, CME programme etc. as a delegate"; no hospitality; no cash or monetary grants in individual capacity. Research funding only through approved institutions and "shall always be fully disclosed".
- **Affiliation (g):** he "may work for pharmaceutical and allied healthcare industries in advisory capacities, as consultants, as researchers" provided integrity is maintained and "such affiliations / employments are fully transparent and disclosed."
- **Endorsement (h):** "A medical practitioner shall not endorse any drug or product of the industry publically." Studies of such products go through scientific bodies or journals.
- Penalties are graded by rupee value: censure from Rs 1,000–5,000; removal from the register for 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and more than 1 year as value rises past Rs 5,000, 10,000, 50,000 and 1,00,000.

**UCPMP 2024 and UCMPMD.** The Department of Pharmaceuticals runs a live "Portal for disclosure of marketing expenditure under UCPMP/UCMPMD" (https://ucmp.pharma-dept.gov.in/), which confirms that companies must report what they spend on doctors and events. The only UCPMP text I could retrieve from the Department's site was the 2014 voluntary version; the 2024 text was not retrievable. From prior knowledge (Confidence: Medium): UCPMP 2024 (March 2024) bars gifts, and bars travel and hospitality to healthcare professionals except where the person is a speaker at a CME/CPD event; allows bona fide consultancy and research engagements under written agreements; caps brand reminders at about Rs 1,000 per item; and requires companies to disclose event expenditure. A parallel code for medical devices (UCMPMD) followed in late 2024. The 2014 text already said "The names or photographs of healthcare professionals must not be used in promotional material" (3.6).

**What this means for speaking and posting**
- Being paid faculty at an industry-supported CME is lawful if contracted and disclosed. Being flown as a delegate is not.
- The company's spend on him is reportable to government. Assume it is discoverable.
- Any talk, post or thread touching a drug class, stent, valve, device or diagnostic where he has a consultancy, trial, proctoring or honorarium relationship needs a visible disclosure.
- No brand names in public-facing content unless clinically unavoidable; never a recommendation of a brand to the lay public.

### 8. International reference norms worth borrowing

- **AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 2.3.2** (retrieved). Six duties: weigh the reputational effect of even personal posts; protect privacy and do not post patient information without appropriate consent; keep patient–physician boundaries online; use privacy settings and monitor what is published about you; "publicly disclose financial interests tied to social media content, such as paid partnerships and corporate sponsorships"; ensure health information shared is "useful and accurate based on professional medical judgment". Confidence: High.
- **GMC (UK), "Using social media as a medical professional"**, in effect 30 January 2024. The GMC site returned HTTP 403. From prior knowledge (Confidence: Medium): the same standards apply online as offline; be honest and do not mislead; state the limits of your knowledge; maintain confidentiality even in closed groups; declare conflicts of interest; when advertising services, information must be factual, checkable and must not exploit vulnerability or lack of medical knowledge. The BMA adds a usable test: do not post anything "you wouldn't want published in a newspaper", and privacy "can never be guaranteed - even in closed groups".
- **ESC.** Ladeiras-Lopes et al., "Social media in cardiovascular medicine: a contemporary review", *European Heart Journal – Digital Health* 2020;1:10–19, from the ESC's Cardiologists of Tomorrow, Digital Health and Media Committees (retrieved). Remove all identifiable data including indirect combinations; no photos or videos of patients or identifiable settings without consent of everyone shown; be cautious answering individual patients' questions; educational-only profiles read as more professional; verify sources before sharing. Confidence: High.
- **ACC.** I could not retrieve an ACC guidance document (JACC returned 403). I recall a 2019 JACC paper by Parwani and colleagues on social media in cardiovascular medicine; could not verify this session. Confidence: Low.

Borrow three things India's rules do not spell out: a standing **conflict-of-interest disclosure line**, the **"newspaper front page" test**, and the **"educational-only profile"** design.

## Draft non-negotiable Rules

1. **Purpose rule.** Every item must pass one question: "Would this be worth publishing if no patient ever came to me because of it?" If not, it is not published. The programme is named and run as public health education and academic outreach.
2. **Identity rule.** Every profile bio and every video description carries: registered name, MBBS MD DM (Cardiology), "Registered with Telangana Medical Council, Reg. No. ____". One spelling of his name everywhere, matching the TGMC register.
3. **No-patient rule.** No patient name, face, voice, story, chat, scan or identifiable detail appears on any public channel. No exceptions for "they agreed" or "they asked me to".
4. **No-testimonial rule.** He does not request, script, incentivise, quote, repost, embed or screenshot any patient review, thanks or story. Replies to reviews are generic and never confirm a clinical relationship.
5. **No-superlative rule.** No "best", "top", "leading", "pioneer", "renowned", success rates, guarantees or procedure-count boasting, by him or by anyone acting for him.
6. **No-endorsement rule.** No product, brand, company, app, lab or insurer is recommended, tagged or featured. No sponsored content. No affiliate links.
7. **No-paid-reach rule.** No ads, boosts, bought followers, paid lists, paid awards or paid rankings.
8. **No call-to-book rule.** Educational content carries no phone number, appointment link, clinic address, fee, offer or "consult me". Contact details live only on the factual profile page.
9. **Scope rule.** He speaks only within cardiology and general cardiovascular prevention. Every factual claim has a guideline or peer-reviewed source noted in the content log.
10. **No-consult-online rule.** Individual clinical questions get one standard reply: general education only; please see your doctor or book a formal consultation. No diagnosis or prescribing in public or in DMs.
11. **Personal-approval rule.** Nothing is published in his name unless he has read and approved the final version. The friend and any AI system draft; the doctor signs off. A log records date, platform, sources, approver.
12. **AI rule.** No patient data ever enters an AI tool. AI-generated images, audio or video are labelled as AI-generated. No AI avatar, cloned voice or synthetic quote of him or of any patient.
13. **Disclosure rule.** Any industry relationship relevant to a topic (consultancy, honorarium, trial, proctoring) is disclosed in the post or slide. A standing "Disclosures" line sits on his profile, even if it reads "None".
14. **Employer rule.** Hospital affiliation is stated factually, never promoted. He declines hospital advertising that features him promotionally, in writing, citing NMC Guidelines 8.3(ii) and 11.5.
15. **Stop rule.** Any show-cause notice, complaint or legal letter pauses all publishing the same day and goes to a lawyer. TGMC notices typically allow 10–15 days to reply; appeals to NMC must be filed within 60 days.

### Consent protocol: the only two lawful uses of patient material

**A. "Patient success stories" for publicity: not available.** Clause 6.2 says consent does not cure a testimonial or promotional patient story. There is no consent form that makes this safe. If a patient chooses, entirely unprompted, to write about their experience on their own account, that is their speech; he does not ask for it, tag it, like it from the practice account, reshare it or quote it.

**B. De-identified cases for scientific or educational purposes: allowed with all of the following.**
1. **Purpose test.** Journal, conference or peer-teaching use. Not lay-facing, not framed as his result.
2. **Who asks.** Someone other than the operating doctor where possible, after the patient is stable or discharged, never before or during the procedure, never while sedated. Care is expressly unaffected by refusal.
3. **Written, specific consent** in the patient's language (Telugu, Hindi, Urdu or English): what material, which outlet, which audience, that online publication cannot be fully withdrawn, the right to refuse and to withdraw for future use, and a contact for complaints. One purpose per form. Signed, dated, witnessed, stored in the hospital record (DPDP s.5–6 standard, applied early).
4. **Hospital permission** for any image leaving hospital systems, through the hospital's process.
5. **De-identification checklist** before export: strip DICOM metadata; remove name, UHID, dates, times, hospital name, accession numbers, operator names; crop faces, tattoos, jewellery, birthmarks; mute audio; alter or omit age, occupation, town and dates in the narrative; check that the combination of details cannot identify the person in Hyderabad.
6. **Second-person check.** A colleague confirms the patient cannot be identified.
7. **Minors and incapacitated adults:** parent or legal guardian consent; default to not using.
8. **Publish in a journal first where feasible;** then share the publication.
9. **Register.** Keep a case-consent register (case code, form location, date, outlet, withdrawal status). On withdrawal, remove what can be removed within 7 days.

## Risks & things to avoid

- **The framing risk.** Internal documents calling this a "PR system to make him widely known" describe exactly what clause 3.2 defines as advertisement ("intended to promote the... reputation"). Name and design the programme around education and academic contribution. Intent is part of the test.
- **Peer complaints.** The Karnataka and Kerala actions began with complaints from professional associations. Visibility attracts complaints from competitors; TGMC now has a standard complaint form. Squeaky-clean beats clever.
- **Hospital marketing done in his name.** The most likely way he breaches is passively: Apollo or Citizens runs a creative, reel or press release about "Dr. Avinash's rare procedure". He is liable if he "knowingly permitted" it (4.4, 11.5).
- **The friend's enthusiasm.** A LinkedIn/X influencer praising his friend's clinical skill to a large audience is a third-party endorsement. So is any engagement tactic (pods, giveaways, follow-for-follow) that looks like manipulated engagement.
- **Angiogram "wins".** Interventional cardiology's native content format (blocked artery, then open artery) is the before/after image the guidelines name. Keep it in journals and closed peer teaching with consent.
- **Review solicitation creep.** A QR code at reception drifts into staff asking happy patients only. That violates Google policy and NMC Explanation V together.
- **Monetisation and paid reach.** Small money, disproportionate risk. Avoid until clarified.
- **AI at scale.** High-volume AI content in a doctor's name raises accuracy, disclosure and "AI-generated promotional campaign" questions. Volume should stay low enough for him to read everything.
- **Stale or unverifiable numbers and titles.** Old procedure counts and unregistered fellowships are precisely what TGMC has suspended doctors for displaying.
- **Do not rely on "everyone does it".** Hyderabad is full of doctor hoardings and testimonial reels. That is the pre-October-2026 market; Telangana Today reports the new rules as aimed at it.
- **Do not treat the 2023 abeyance as a loophole.** The parts that mattered have been reissued.
