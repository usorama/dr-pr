# 19 — The lighthouse: prevention at population scale, lifelong value for patients, and teaching the next generation of cardiologists

**Executive summary**

1. **Information alone rarely changes a biomarker.** Nine negative or neutral trials (Stanford Five-City, DISHA, SMARThealth India in West Godavari, mWellcare, TEXTMEDS, an Indian WhatsApp reminder trial, two US workplace-wellness RCTs, the SI! school trial) set against the ones that worked (North Karelia, PROLIFIC in Kerala, the Kerala self-help-group trial, China's village-doctor trial, CARRS, salt substitution) show one pattern: education works when it is attached to **measurement, a route to treatment, repeated human follow-up, a family or peer unit, and a changed environment**.
2. **The Indian gap is control, not knowledge of what to do.** Nine modifiable factors explain 86% of heart-attack risk in South Asians, who have their first infarct about six years earlier (53.0 vs 58.8 years). Yet only 12.6% of Indian hypertensives are controlled, only 27% of treated Indian coronary patients reach LDL-C ≤50 mg/dL, and statin use falls from 99.7% to 70% within six months of angioplasty.
3. **Every risk calculator under-calls Indians.** In 4,975 Indian first-heart-attack patients, no model had flagged more than about one in five as high risk beforehand. A public programme should therefore lead with "know five numbers and count your risk factors" (the CSI 2024 and LAI 2023 method), and use calculators as a second lens only.
4. **The trainee audience is larger than assumed and badly served online.** India admits about 1,080 cardiology trainees a year (592 DM seats in 2026-27; about 485 DrNB seats in the 2025 matrix); Telangana alone has 32 DM and 55 DrNB seats, the latter in 24 Hyderabad-area hospitals. Society YouTube channels are tiny (CSI HQ: 68 subscribers); the best-known open Indian resources are one-man efforts (Dr S Venkatesan's blog, 6.77 million hits; Johnson's Cardiology, 59,200 subscribers).
5. **The lighthouse model** proposed here has three flagships on about 4 hours a week: a Telugu/English **Heart School** with measured 100-day cohorts; a **patients-for-life** system (heart passport, 30/90/365-day checks, an honest stable-angina decision aid); and an open weekly **case conference plus atlas** for trainees. Each publishes its numbers, including the disappointing ones.

**1.3 Primordial prevention, lifetime risk and cumulative LDL**

- Primordial prevention means stopping risk factors from appearing (childhood diet, activity, tobacco-free adolescence), as distinct from primary prevention (treating risk factors) [BK, High]. Fuster's FAMILIA trial in Harlem preschools improved a knowledge-attitudes-habits score (2.86 points) but measured no clinical outcome (PMID 31023422; High).
- The cumulative-exposure concept ("cholesterol-years") is the scientific basis for the Indian slogans "earlier the better, lower the better, longer the better" (CSI 2024, quoted from the executive summary, PMID 38052658) and for LAI's emphasis on "lifetime ASCVD risk instead of the conventional 10-year risk" (PMID 38485619; High).
- This is the single most teachable idea for a 30-year-old IT employee: a moderately high LDL for 25 years does more damage than a high LDL found and treated at 55.

**2.3 Workplaces**

- **BJ's Wholesale Club** (160 worksites, 32,974 employees): at 18 months more employees reported regular exercise (+8.3 points) and weight management (+13.6 points); **no effect on 10 clinical markers, 38 spending measures, absenteeism or job performance**. Same at three years (PMID 30990549; 34097526; High).
- **Illinois Workplace Wellness Study** (4,834 employees): strong selection (participants were already healthier and cheaper); no effect on spending, behaviours, productivity or biometrics after two years; confidence intervals "rule out 84% of previous estimates" (PMID 31564754; 32453346; High).
- **Indian industrial worksites** (Prabhakaran et al., JACC 2009): a demonstration project reporting improved risk factors at intervention sites [BK, Medium; abstract not retrievable]. The INDIA-WORKS diabetes-prevention trial across 11 worksites has published its adaptation process; **main results could not be found** (Research Square preprint, doi 10.21203/rs.3.rs-3143470/v1).
- Local need is documented in files 04 and 09: 84% fatty liver and 76.5% high LDL among 345 Hyderabad IT employees (Scientific Reports 2025); about 46% with three or more metabolic risk factors at a mean age of 30 (NIN, Nutrients 2023).

Reading: generic wellness modules do not move numbers. An employer programme is defensible only if it measures, links abnormal results to treatment and changes the canteen or the working day.

**2.4 Schools**

- **HRIDAY-CATCH** (Delhi): intervention students less likely to be offered, try or intend to use tobacco (PMID 12018753; High).
- **Project MYTRI** (Delhi and Chennai): students less likely to increase cigarette or bidi smoking over two years (PMID 19299670; High).
- **SI! Program, Spain** (1,326 adolescents, 24 schools): small gain at 2 years (0.44 points) that **disappeared at 4 years**; "neutral effect" (PMID 37531100; High).
- **Hridayamrit Foundation** (American Journal of Preventive Cardiology 2026): a youth-driven Indian model with a national-to-campus training cascade, CPR training, screening camps and pre-specified metrics; outcome data are early (PMC13326072; High that it exists, Low on effect).
- HRIDAY-SHAN's later evaluations: **not retrieved this session**.

Tobacco prevention in schools has Indian trial support. General "heart health" lessons do not yet have durable outcome evidence.

**2.5 Social media, nudges and commitment devices**

- Systematic review of social-media interventions in low- and middle-income countries: 33 studies; 22 claimed effectiveness, but only 13 measured engagement and few used a behaviour-change model (PMID 35436220; High).
- Review of 16 studies (7,372 people) on weight and activity: "evidence … is mixed"; engagement did not reliably predict change (PMID 36927627; High).
- A 2025 scoping review of 43 reviews: most programmes are one-way spread (37 of 43); fewer use conversation (21) or peer motivation (11) (PMID 40540738; High).
- **HeartStrong** (1,509 MI survivors): electronic pill bottles, lottery incentives and a support partner produced **no difference** in adherence, readmission or cost (PMID 28654972; High).
- **NUDGE-FLU** (964,870 Danish adults): letters stressing cardiovascular benefit, or repeated, raised vaccination by under one percentage point; seven other letter designs did nothing (PMID 36871213; High).
- Commitment contracts: a Philippine bank trial (Giné, Karlan, Zinman, 2010) found smokers offered a deposit contract were about 3 percentage points more likely to pass a nicotine test at 6 and 12 months [BK, Medium; paper not retrieved; doi 10.1257/app.2.4.213].
- Decision aids are the best-evidenced "information" tool: knowledge +11.9 points out of 100, accurate risk perception RR 1.94, consultation only 1.5 minutes longer, no increase in regret (Cochrane 2024, 209 studies; PMID 38284415; High).

**2.6 What turns information into behaviour**

Across the trials above, the programmes that changed outcomes share five ingredients. The ones that failed usually had one or two.

1. **A measurement the person owns** (BP, LDL, HbA1c, waist, tobacco status), repeated.
2. **A route to treatment** that does not depend on the person's initiative: titration protocol, free or cheap drugs, a named clinic (CRHCP, HOPE 4, barbershop, CARRS, IHCI).
3. **Repeated human contact** over 6–24 months from someone trusted: health worker, peer leader, pharmacist (SPREAD, PROLIFIC, Kerala SHG).
4. **A social unit**: family (PROLIFIC, HOPE 4's treatment supporter) or peer group (SHG, barbershop).
5. **An environmental default**: the salt in the kitchen (SSaSS), one pill instead of three (SECURE), tobacco policy (North Karelia).

A YouTube channel supplies none of the five by itself. It supplies reach and trust, which are the entry point to a programme that has them.

### 3. Programme designs a single doctor could lead

**3.1 "Know your numbers"**

The evidence base is May Measurement Month (68% of 362,708 Indians screened had never had a BP reading) and the self-monitoring meta-analysis (measurement helps only with a co-intervention). A workable public message is **five numbers and one lifetime test**: blood pressure, LDL or non-HDL cholesterol, fasting glucose or HbA1c, waist, tobacco (yes or no), plus Lp(a) once. Each number gets a target taken from CSI 2024 and a stated next step.

The decisive fact: in the 2026 study, "80% of patients were still not identified as high risk before their event" by any model. A self-assessment page must say this in plain words and must not tell anyone they are "safe".

**3.3 Family-based cascade screening**

PROLIFIC is the template and it is Indian: start from the patient with coronary disease before 55, enrol first-degree relatives and the spouse, screen, run structured sessions, link to a primary-care clinic, follow up actively. Result: 64% vs 46% reached the composite target at two years. For lipids, file 09 records that about 15% of north Indian premature-CAD patients had potential familial hypercholesterolaemia and that 66% of tested relatives carried the family mutation. Lp(a) carries the highest population burden in South Asians (PAR 9.5%). This is the one prevention programme that begins inside his own consulting room and needs no camp.

**3.4 Employer programmes for the IT corridor**

Design implied by the two null RCTs and the Indian data:
- Voluntary, with results going to the employee only (aggregate, anonymous report to the employer).
- A **measured baseline** (BP twice, lipid profile, HbA1c, waist, tobacco) by an accredited laboratory paid by the employer.
- A 100-day cohort with a re-measurement at day 100 and at 12 months; a canteen and meeting-room change agreed with HR (salt, fried snacks, stairs, standing meetings).
- A written referral route for abnormal results to **the employee's own doctor or any physician**, not to him (NMC clause 8.1(ii) on soliciting; see files 11 and 15).
- Entry through HYSEA's HR council or the SCSC Healthcare Forum (file 15).

**3.6 Cohort-based prevention challenges**

A "100-day heart reset": 50–150 people per cohort, enrolled through one employer, apartment association or alumni group. Baseline numbers, weekly lesson, a peer leader per ten participants (the K-DPP and SHG model), a family partner, a day-100 re-measurement, a 12-month check. K-DPP cost US$22.5 a head for 15 sessions; that is the right order of magnitude for budgeting.

**3.7 Measuring "cardiologist visits avoided" credibly**

No programme of this size can observe heart attacks avoided. What can be reported honestly:

| Level | Metric | How |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | People who completed a lesson; handout downloads | Platform analytics |
| Knowledge | Pre/post quiz score | 8 questions per cohort |
| Action | Share who measured all five numbers; share who involved a relative | Self-report with upload |
| Control | Share at BP target; mean systolic change; share at LDL goal; verified tobacco quit at 6 months | Re-measurement by a laboratory |
| Modelled events averted | Apply published effect per unit change to the cohort's measured change and baseline risk | Method below |
| Appropriate care | Share of high-risk people who saw any doctor within 30 days | Follow-up call |

Modelling method (state every assumption in the report):
- Baseline 10-year risk from the WHO South Asia chart for each participant, with a note that it under-calls.
- Effect sizes from Ettehad (RR 0.80 per 10 mm Hg) and CTT (about 22% per 1 mmol/L LDL), scaled log-linearly to the measured change, discounted for the share who maintain the change at 12 months.
- **Illustration only:** 1,000 hypertensive adults at 10% ten-year risk expect about 100 events. A sustained net fall of 4.5 mm Hg (the size seen in the Kerala SHG, COBRA-BPS and Indian salt trials) gives RR about 0.90, so about 10 events averted over ten years, if the fall is sustained and if the trials' effects transfer.
- Always report the before-after change **without** a control as "change", not "effect". Regression to the mean inflates before-after gains in people selected for high readings (both arms of mWellcare fell 13 mm Hg). Where possible, use a wait-list cohort as comparison, and ask IIPH Hyderabad or ICMR-NIN to co-design the evaluation.
- The phrase to use: "people who reached their targets", never "heart attacks prevented".

## B. Lifelong value for patients

### 4. Evidence-based support after an event

**4.1 Cardiac rehabilitation**

- Cochrane 2026 update (107 trials, 26,886 patients): at 6–12 months, MI **RR 0.72** (high certainty), all-cause hospital admission RR 0.65, all-cause mortality RR 0.86 (0.74–1.00); cost-effective in eight economic studies. Twenty-six trials were in low- and middle-income countries (PMID 42754260; High).
- Cochrane 2021: cardiovascular mortality RR 0.77 at medium term and 0.58 at long term (PMID 34741536; High).
- India: 23 programmes found, one place per 360 eligible patients a year, top barrier "lack of patient referral" (PMID 32489801; High; data from about 2017). An Indian expert consensus on hybrid (centre plus home) rehabilitation appeared in 2026 and a JAPI review in 2025 calls for tele-rehabilitation (PMID 42147577; 41391089; High that they exist).
- The referral is the doctor's act. An interventionalist who refers every stent patient to a structured programme (centre, hybrid or Yoga-CaRe style) is already ahead of practice.

**4.2 Secondary-prevention adherence in India**

| Finding | Source, confidence |
|---|---|
| Registry of 716 post-ACS patients, 99.7% treated by angioplasty: at 6 months aspirin 97.3% to 83.9%, dual antiplatelets 99.7% to 70.2%, **statins 99.7% to 70.0%**, **high-intensity statin 92.0% to 45.7%**; good adherence in 42.7%; 20.5% lost to follow-up | PMID 40020999 (Indian Heart Journal 2025); High |
| Maharashtra insurance-scheme PCI cohort (2,064 patients): 22.8% stopped dual antiplatelets early within a year; continuing was associated with lower one-year mortality (adjusted HR 0.52) | BMC Cardiovasc Disord 2022, doi 10.1186/s12872-022-02677-8; Medium (search summary) |
| Rural Kerala community sample of 436 coronary patients: medication adherence 56.4% | PMID 41451041; High |
| PURE (2003–09): among people with coronary disease in low-income countries (India included), statins 3.3%, antiplatelets 8.8% | PMID 21872920; High, old |
| Health-worker home visits for a year raised adherence (97% vs 92%) and smoking cessation (85% vs 52%) | SPREAD, PMID 26857999; High |
| A polypill improved adherence and cut events by 24% after MI | SECURE, PMID 36018037; High |

**4.3 Heart failure self-care**

- Trivandrum registry: one-year mortality 30.8%, five-year mortality 59%, median survival 3.1 years; only 25% of HFrEF patients left hospital on guideline-directed therapy (PMID 28625377; 33049297; High).
- **TIME-HF (India, Circulation 2026; 1,507 patients):** nurse-coordinated, digitally supported collaborative care: deaths 21.6% vs 26.7%, HR 0.78 (0.63–0.95); probability of two years alive and out of hospital 46.5% vs 34.7% (PMID 42668439; High). File 15 already builds "After the stent" on this trial.
- Teach-back at discharge: meta-analysis of 6 studies, readmission OR 0.40 (0.17–0.94), low-quality evidence (PMID 36411152; Medium).
- A South Indian survey found poor heart-failure knowledge and self-care (PMID 39703650; High).

**4.4 Shared decision-making and the stable-angina conversation**

- **ISCHEMIA** (5,179 patients with moderate or severe ischaemia): an initial invasive strategy did not reduce ischaemic events or death over 3.2 years (5-year rate 16.4% vs 18.2%; deaths 145 vs 144) (PMID 32227755; High).
- **ORBITA** (200 patients, sham-controlled): angioplasty did not increase exercise time more than placebo (+16.6 s, not significant) (PMID 29103656; High).
- **ORBITA-2** (301 patients on little or no antianginal drug): angioplasty **did** relieve angina better than placebo (symptom score 2.9 vs 5.6; OR 2.21) (PMID 38015442; High).
- What patients believe: in a multi-site US study, 90% thought elective angioplasty would extend life and 88% that it would prevent heart attacks; 1% identified symptom relief as the only benefit (PMID 25200209; High).
- Tools: Mayo's Statin Choice aid improved knowledge and risk perception in a Spanish trial but was opened by only 51% of clinicians even when embedded in the record (PMID 26343571; 28797295; High). A UK digital aid for stable angina (CONNECT) has been user-tested, not yet trialled (PMID 35624456; High). ACC's CardioSmart publishes decision aids, fact sheets, action plans and infographics (cardiosmart.org; High).
- No Telugu decision aid for stable angina or statins was found (not searched exhaustively; Low–Medium).

An interventional cardiologist who publishes an honest Telugu aid ("a stent in stable angina relieves chest pain; it has not been shown to prevent heart attacks or lengthen life; tablets are the first step") is doing something rare and is fully inside the NMC's education lane. It is also the content most likely to be trusted, because it argues against his own procedural interest.

**4.5 Patient-held records and second opinions**

- Patient-held records: a systematic review in cancer care found randomised trials showed "an absence of effect" while patients liked them (PMID 17324196; High, old, other specialty). A glaucoma record did not improve knowledge (PMID 28165608). So a "heart passport" should be justified as safety and continuity (stent type and date, ejection fraction, drug list, LDL target, next review), not as proven to improve outcomes, and it should be audited.
- Second opinions: reviews are thin. In oncology a second opinion changed diagnosis, treatment or prognosis in 12–69% of cases and confirmed the first in 43–82% (PMID 26687791; High). **No Indian cardiology data found.** A popular Indian channel named "Cardiac Second Opinion" has 333,000 YouTube subscribers (Invidious listing; Medium), which shows demand.
- Practical stance: tell every patient facing an elective procedure that a second opinion is welcome and hand over the angiogram disc and report unasked. This costs nothing and is a visible marker of confidence.

- **Open access by principle**, stated in writing (Venkatesan; LITFL's Creative Commons licence).

- **Learners become authors** (CardioNerds' fellow-written cases).
- **No sponsor in the frame.**

The standing criticism is uneven quality without peer review; a rapid review lists appraisal tools (PMID 32560781). His answer to that criticism is to cite the guideline or trial on every teaching slide and to keep a public errata page.

### 7. Mentoring design

**7.1 What Indian evidence says about mentorship programmes**

- Undergraduate mentorship in a Chhattisgarh medical college: students agree it is essential, but confidence in the existing programme is limited by "biases, fears, and misinformation" (PMID 38022138; High).
- "Exponere", a peer research-mentorship programme run from Seth GSMC and KEM Hospital: 64 students; knowledge, attitudes and research skills improved (preprint, doi 10.1101/2025.04.03.25325199; Medium).
- Faculty plus near-peer mentoring for burnout in Indian medical students (Bioinformation 2026; abstract not available; Low).
- Tele-mentoring on the ECHO model improved knowledge and practice scores of community health officers in Telangana and Tripura (PMID 41104324; High). File 09 notes ECHO India lists no cardiology programme.

Design implications: selection must be open and visibly fair; the mentor's role must be defined (not an examiner, not an employer); near-peer layers work; each mentee needs a concrete output.

**7.2 What a "school of thought" requires**

1. **A codified approach**: a written sequence that others can follow (for example, a fixed pre-procedure decision checklist, or a fixed way of reading an angiogram alongside the patient's goals).
2. **Published outcomes** from using it: an audit or registry, with denominators.
3. **A text**: an open atlas or handbook that states the method and is cited.
4. **A recurring forum**: weekly conference, then an annual course.
5. **Alumni**: people who trained in it, publish with it and teach it.
6. **Independence**: the method does not depend on one device, drug or hospital.

Naming it comes last. A name without items 1–5 reads as branding, and under the NMC guidelines branding a doctor's "skills or achievements" is advertising.

### Measurement

- **One dashboard, four rows:** reach; action (numbers logged, relatives tested); control (at-target rates in cohorts and in his patients); teaching (sessions, attendees, atlas cases, mentee outputs).
- **Denominators always shown.** "112 of 160 completed day 100" rather than "112 people improved".
- **Modelled impact labelled as modelled**, with the method in section 3.7.
- **Null results published.** If cohort 1 shows no fall in BP, that is reported and the design is changed. The literature above is full of honest nulls; adding one is a mark of seriousness.

### Principles that keep it authentic

1. **Teach what you practise.** Nothing goes into a lesson that he does not do with his own patients; nothing is taught to trainees that his own audit contradicts.
2. **Numbers before opinions.** Every claim carries its source and its size.
3. **Publish outcomes, including the unflattering ones.**
4. **Credit others by name.** PROLIFIC, the Kerala SHG trial, TIME-HF, Dr Venkatesan's blog, Dr Kamath's network and STEMI India are the shoulders; say so.
5. **No fear.** State risk as "x in 100 over 10 years", never as threat. No "silent killer" thumbnails.
6. **Argue against your own interest when the evidence does.** The stable-angina aid is the proof.
7. **Free, open and sponsor-free.** No company logo on public or trainee teaching; any society-run workshop discloses its funding.
8. **Privacy is absolute.** No patient is identifiable anywhere, with or without consent.
9. **Correct errors in public,** on a dated errata page.
10. **He approves everything; AI and helpers assist.**

## Risks & things to avoid

- **Over-claiming prevention.** "Heart attacks prevented" or "lives saved" cannot be shown by an uncontrolled cohort. Use "reached target". Remember regression to the mean.
- **False reassurance from calculators.** All models missed about 80% of Indian first-MI patients. Never label a person "low risk, no action".
- **Screening harms and the NMC.** Camps are the riskiest activity under the 2026 guidelines (file 15). Abnormal results must go to the person's own doctor; no free procedures, no referral to himself, no packages.
- **Salt substitutes are not for everyone.** Potassium-enriched salt can be dangerous in chronic kidney disease and with potassium-sparing drugs; SSaSS excluded such people [BK, High]. Every mention needs this caveat.
- **Drug and product talk.** Teach classes and targets, not brands. Polypill evidence can be explained; a named product cannot be endorsed (file 02).
- **Individual advice in public channels.** Comments asking "should I take a statin?" must be answered with general education and "see your doctor"; anything else risks breaching the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines.
- **Patients' group drift.** A WhatsApp broadcast list for education is defensible; a group where patients post thanks, reviews or each other's details is not. From May 2027 the DPDP consent and notice duties apply.
- **Teaching material.** Angiograms and ECGs carry names, dates and hospital numbers in the image; strip them, change non-essential details, and follow the tiered consent protocol in file 11. Hospital ownership of images must be cleared in writing.
- **The stable-angina message will annoy some peers.** Present it as what ISCHEMIA, ORBITA and ORBITA-2 found, including that angioplasty does relieve angina, and never as a comment on other doctors.
- **Industry money in teaching.** The one comparable Indian series lists a pharmaceutical supporter. Staying sponsor-free is the differentiator; one sponsored session would spend it.
- **A named "method" too early** reads as self-promotion and falls inside the NMC definition of advertisement.
- **Time.** Three flagships at four hours a week work only if the passport calls, editing, translation and scheduling are delegated. If time halves, keep the patient system and the weekly case; pause cohorts.
- **Workplace programmes that only "raise awareness"** have two large null RCTs against them. Do not lend his name to one.
- **Crowded Telugu video field.** Dr Mukharjee's channel is near 500,000 subscribers (file 13). Compete on measured cohorts, written aids and trainee teaching, not on daily Shorts.

## Open questions / gaps

1. **His own baseline.** How many ACS/PCI patients he treats a year, what share return at 12 months, his current LDL-at-goal rate, and whether either hospital has a rehabilitation programme he can refer to. Without these the 36-month targets are guesses.
2. **Trainee communities.** Names and sizes of the Telegram/WhatsApp groups and apps used for NEET-SS and DM/DrNB exit preparation, and the colleges behind Telangana's 32 DM seats. Needs someone inside (a current resident) rather than web search.
3. **Formal teaching routes for a non-institutional cardiologist.** Primary text of NMC's 2025 faculty regulations on visiting or part-time faculty; any Telangana order; NBEMS Joint Accreditation terms; UCPMP 2024 rules for society-run workshops.
4. **Risk prediction for Indians.** Which SCORE2 Asia-Pacific region India falls in; whether any prospective Indian cohort has validated QRISK3 or PREVENT; whether CSI or ICMR is building an Indian score. This decides what the public self-assessment page should show.
5. **Evaluation partner and design.** Whether IIPH Hyderabad, ICMR-NIN or a university department would co-design a wait-list-controlled evaluation of the 100-day cohort, and whether INDIA-WORKS has published its main results (not found).
6. Not researched for lack of budget: HRIDAY-SHAN outcome data; mCessation results in India; Telugu-language decision aids already in existence; ABDM/ABHA personal-health-record uptake as a carrier for the passport; evidence on AI coaching for risk-factor control (covered by the AI stream).
