# 15 — Community programmes made executable: CPR/AED, STEMI care and the under-researched causes

**Executive summary**

1. **The fastest route to a credible CPR programme runs through his own second hospital.** Citizens Specialty Hospital, Nallagandla, states on its own site that it is an American Heart Association (AHA) authorised International Training Centre offering BLS/ACLS provider *and instructor* courses (BLS Rs 3,500 in Aug 2025). Lay hands-only CPR needs no certification at all; the Indian layperson protocol (COLS) was designed for it. The Indian Resuscitation Council Federation (IRCF) route could not be costed because its site returns no content.
2. **Telangana's 2023 plan (1 lakh trained, 1,200–1,400 AEDs) still has no published delivery figures.** What exists in 2024–26 is piecemeal: 2,000+ metro staff trained (2023, no AEDs mentioned), 1,248 city police (Jan 2025), a Resuscitation Academy at EMRI Green Health Services (Mar 2025), and one Freemasons' lodge at 758 of 1,000 trained. No AED registry for India was found. The western corridor is open ground.
3. **STEMI-Telangana has published nothing since Dec 2022**, while Tamil Nadu (71,907 patients, WhatsApp-linked hubs), Goa (door-to-needle 18 min) and Himachal Pradesh (thrombolysis at spokes 27.6% to 65.5% after weekly virtual teaching) have. The Himachal design — weekly teaching plus a WhatsApp ECG group — is exactly what one cardiologist can offer. Mass-media chest-pain campaigns mostly fail to shorten delay (REACT trial); teaching first-contact clinicians works better.
4. **The NMC's 6 Oct 2026 Guidelines do not contain the word "camp".** I read all 13 pages. Training and education sit under clause 5.1 and the 7.3 exemption for government campaigns; the danger clauses are 8.1(ii) (soliciting, third-party marketing), 8.1(x) (free procedures as inducement) and 8.3(ii)/9.2 (hospital "promotional portrayal of an individual RMP"). CPR training is the cleanest activity; screening camps are the riskiest.
5. **Three programmes are specified below to run-of-show level:** (A) "Hands that save" — 1,000 people hands-on CPR-trained in 12 months for Rs 1.4–2.6 lakh (about Rs 30,000 if kit is borrowed); (B) "First ECG" — a 12-session chest-pain/ECG series for first-contact doctors for under Rs 50,000 a year; (C) "After the stent" — a monthly patient and family education circle modelled on the TIME-HF trial (India, 2026: mortality hazard ratio 0.78 with nurse-coordinated care).

## Findings

### 1. CPR training routes in Hyderabad

**1.1 Does a lay "hands-only" session need certification? No.**

- No Indian statute, NMC rule or state order regulating who may teach or perform lay CPR was found. The 2026 NMC Guidelines are silent on it. (High confidence that nothing was found; Medium that nothing exists.)
- COLS (Compression-Only Life Support) is the Indian layperson protocol and is taught by "certified instructors for resuscitation courses from the Indian Resuscitation Council" using IRC-validated material. A 300-person study of ambulance drivers, security guards and housekeeping staff in Ahmedabad used groups of 10–15, 30–45 minutes of teaching, then supervised manikin practice; correct answers on "first step on collapse" rose from 20.2% to 95%. No skill test and no retention test were reported. (PMC10104097; Medium.)
- Hyderabad programmes that train the public issue participation certificates or none (Gandhi alumni camp, Lodge Keys, Project Pulse; see file 09). (High.)
- Practical position: he does not need a certificate to teach compressions. Certification matters for **credibility of his volunteer instructors** and for anyone who will teach healthcare workers.

**1.4 Legal protection for bystanders**

- **Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act 2019, s.134A.** PRS summary: a Good Samaritan is "a person who renders emergency medical or non-medical assistance to a victim at the scene of an accident", acting in good faith, voluntarily and without expectation of reward, and "will not be liable for any civil or criminal action for any injury to or death of an accident victim". (PRS; High.) The section itself, as I recall it, covers "the victim of an accident involving a motor vehicle" and removes liability even where the injury or death resulted from the Good Samaritan's negligence in acting or failing to act. **India Code returned HTTP 403, so the section text was not re-read this round — verify before quoting.**
- **The limit that matters:** s.134A is about road-accident victims. A collapse at home, in an office or in a gym is outside its words. The 2016 Supreme Court-backed Good Samaritan guidelines (30 March 2016) are likewise framed around road accidents (Wikipedia; Medium).
- For non-road emergencies the protection is general criminal-law good-faith exceptions (Indian Penal Code ss.88 and 92; I believe the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita equivalents are ss.26 and 30 — **background knowledge; get a lawyer to confirm section numbers**). No Indian law protecting lay AED users specifically was found; a law-firm blog says there is "no comprehensive national AED law" and no legal requirement to be trained before using one (advocategandhi.com, carries an AI-generated-content disclaimer; Low).
- The 2026 Indian expert statement on school CPR asks for "Good Samaritan protection and indemnity clauses" to be written into any CPR law — i.e. the authors regard present protection as incomplete (PMC13069308; High).
- **How to say it in a session (accurate and reassuring):** "Indian law protects people who help road-accident victims in good faith. For a collapse elsewhere, the general rule that an act done in good faith to save a life is not an offence applies. No case of a bystander being prosecuted for attempting CPR was found in this research." Do not promise blanket immunity.

**1.6 What format works — evidence**

| Finding | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| A 22-minute video with a personal manikin (30-minute self-instruction) taught CPR to adults aged 40–70 as well as or better than a multi-hour Heartsaver course; n=285 | Lynch 2005, *Resuscitation* | High |
| Skills decay fast either way: at 2 months, overall adequate performance fell from 60% to 44% (self-trained) and from 42% to 30% (Heartsaver) | Einspruch 2007, *Resuscitation* | High |
| An ultra-brief compression-only video shown to mall shoppers (n=100): more called for help (31 percentage-point difference), compression rate up by a median 19/min, hands-off time down 27 s; depth no better | Panchal 2014, *Resuscitation* | High |
| Skills decline measurably within 6–12 months; refreshers of about 5 minutes every few months hold retention better than an annual workshop | 2026 Indian expert consensus, *Indian Pacing Electrophysiol J* | Medium–High |
| "Kids Save Lives" (WHO-endorsed 2015): 2 hours a year from age 12; AED by 14; teachers as trainers; "check–call–compress" | Böttiger 2015; PMC13069308 | High |
| Denmark 2001–2010: bystander CPR 21.1% to 44.9%; 30-day survival 3.5% to 10.8% (n=19,468), alongside mandatory school and driving-licence training. Authors caution that causality is uncertain | Wissenberg 2013, *JAMA* | High |
| Lodge Keys/EMRI GHS Hyderabad format: 3.5 hours, capped at 40, Rs 1,000 requested from those who can pay | Business News This Week, 3 Jun 2026 | High |

Design consequences: short and hands-heavy beats long and lecture-heavy; depth is the weak point, so at least one feedback manikin is worth its price; build a five-minute refresher into month 6; give every trainee a video to take home and teach from.

**1.7 Session design for 30–50 people in 75 minutes (my design, built on the above)**

- Ratio: 1 manikin per 4–5 people, 1 instructor per 2 manikins. For 40 people: 8 basic + 2 feedback manikins, 2 AED trainers, 5 instructors.
- 0–5 min: sign-in by QR or paper, privacy notice, where the nearest AED is.
- 5–15: recognise (unresponsive, not breathing normally), shout for help, call 108 on speaker.
- 15–22: instructor demonstration of compressions and the COLS sequence.
- 22–52: stations. Everyone does two 2-minute rounds; each person passes once through a feedback manikin for rate and depth.
- 52–62: AED trainer drill in groups of five: switch on, follow prompts, pads, stand clear.
- 62–72: skills tick-list signed by the station instructor (five items: checks response, calls 108, hand position, rate 100–120, adequate depth and recoil).
- 72–75: two-question feedback, take-home video link, six-month refresher opt-in.

**3.2 Delay, and what reduces it**

- Symptom-to-first-contact is about 60–120 min in high-income countries and often 300–480 min in India and similar settings; fewer than 25–30% use an ambulance; "invisible" delay arises after first contact at non-PCI clinics (late ECG, mis-triage). (Clin Cardiol 2026 review, PMC13239337; High.)
- **Public campaigns have a weak record.** REACT, 20 US cities: no difference in delay (P=.54), ambulance use up 20% (JAMA 2000). An Australian national campaign: no change (1993). "Call fast, Call 911" mail campaign: no overall effect, but more 911 use among people with a previous MI (1997). Two European campaigns did shorten delay (Göteborg: median 3 h to 2 h 40; Geneva: median 180 to 155 min) (Eur Heart J 1992; Heart 1996). The 2026 review's verdict: awareness campaigns alone give "limited/short-term effect". (High.)
- Consequence: put effort into (a) first-contact clinicians and (b) people already at high risk — his own patients and their families — rather than general "know the signs" publicity.

**5.2 What an employed consultant must clear with the hospital** (his contract was not available; this is the checklist to put in writing)

1. Permission for outside teaching in his own name and time, at both hospitals.
2. Whose name appears where. Under 8.2(iii) he speaks "in their own name and designation, without promoting their employer organization"; under 8.3(ii) hospital communication must not contain "promotional portrayal of an individual RMP"; 9.2 bars use of his photograph where it is "self-advertisement, solicitation or promotional publicity". So: no hospital-designed poster with his face; no doctor-branded event names.
3. Kit loan and staff volunteer time (nurses, technicians) — on or off duty, and who insures them off site.
4. Use of hospital rooms for Programme C and whether it counts as a clinical service (documentation, nursing governance).
5. Access to de-identified ECGs and aggregate patient data; ethics-committee route for any audit intended for publication.
6. A written line that no programme will distribute appointment cards, discount vouchers or package leaflets.

### Programme B — "First ECG": chest-pain and ECG upskilling for first-contact doctors

**Goal.** Twelve monthly sessions reaching 60–80 general practitioners, casualty medical officers and nurses within about 15 km of his hospitals and, by invitation, government spoke-hospital officers. Measured by pre/post quiz gain and by the number of clinics that adopt a written chest-pain pathway (ECG within 10 minutes; a named transfer destination).

**Partners.** The local IMA branch and Telangana Medical Council CPD process for credit hours (file 07 notes an organiser's declaration proforma exists); CSI Telangana chapter for endorsement; the STEMI-Telangana nodal officer (offer the series free to spoke doctors); PHFI training division (regional-faculty application); a colleague from WINCARS/NIMS for the session on women.

**Format.** 60–75 minutes online on a fixed weekday evening, with one in-person half-day each quarter. Ten real, de-identified ECGs per session; for each, the participant commits to "thrombolyse, transfer or reassure" before the answer. A clinician WhatsApp group for de-identified ECG questions between sessions, mirroring the Himachal model.

**Suggested 12-month syllabus** (his to edit)

| Month | Topic |
|---|---|
| 1 | The 10-minute ECG and the normal trace |
| 2 | ST-elevation MI: anterior, inferior, lateral |
| 3 | Mimics: early repolarisation, pericarditis, LVH, bundle-branch block |
| 4 | High-risk patterns without classic ST elevation; posterior and right-sided leads |
| 5 | Non-ST-elevation syndromes; troponin in a clinic |
| 6 | Chest pain when no ECG is available; red flags |
| 7 | Thrombolyse or transfer: windows, contraindications, pharmaco-invasive care |
| 8 | Rhythms a clinic must not miss |
| 9 | Heart failure in primary care |
| 10 | After the event: drugs and targets the GP maintains |
| 11 | Young patients and women: different presentations, same urgency |
| 12 | Participants present their own clinic audit |

**Kit and cost (my estimates).** Video-meeting licence about Rs 15,000 a year; printed pathway cards for clinics (500 at about Rs 15) Rs 7,500; quarterly in-person room and tea about Rs 5,000 x 4; CPD accreditation fee not known. Total under Rs 50,000. No pharmaceutical sponsorship.

**Roles.** He prepares and teaches (3 h/month). The helper handles invitations, quiz forms and attendance (3 h/month). A cardiology registrar or fellow de-identifies ECGs. One GP co-moderator keeps the level right.

**Data.** Attendance by session; pre/post quiz score; three-month follow-up form (does the clinic have an ECG machine, median self-reported time to ECG, where patients are sent); number of pathway cards adopted. Year 2: door-in to door-out times from two willing clinics.

### Programme C — "After the stent": a patient and family education circle

**Goal.** A standing monthly session for people who have had a heart attack, stent, bypass or heart-failure admission, and one family member each. Year-1 targets: 120 patients enrolled; at 12 months, the proportion with LDL and blood pressure at goal, on guideline-recommended drugs, tobacco-free, and with a family member trained in CPR (through Programme A).

**Evidence base.** TIME-HF components (risk stratification, drug optimisation, nurse coordination, self-care education, active follow-up); Yoga-CaRe for a low-cost activity module; the Cochrane finding that text reminders alone are unproven (file 09), so messaging is support, not the intervention.

**Partners.** The hospital's cardiac nursing lead, physiotherapy and dietetics; a clinical pharmacist; a yoga therapist familiar with post-MI patients; the hospital ethics committee if results will be published. It is open to patients of any cardiologist in the hospital — that keeps it a service, not a personal following.

**Format.** 90 minutes, same weekend slot each month, in a hospital seminar room or a community hall. 20 minutes teaching, 20 minutes skill (pulse, home BP, reading a prescription, weighing and fluid diary), 30 minutes small-group questions led by the nurse, 20 minutes activity or family topic. A broadcast-only message list (opt-in, one message a week, no individual advice).

**Twelve-month cycle**

| Month | Topic |

| 1 | What happened to my heart; what a stent does and does not do |
| 2 | The medicines that matter and why stopping early is dangerous |
| 3 | Warning signs, calling 108, and the family learns CPR |

| 5 | Activity, work, driving, intimacy; a guided yoga-based session |
| 6 | Heart failure self-care: weight, fluid, sick-day rules |
| 7 | Know your numbers: LDL, BP, sugar |
| 8 | Tobacco and alcohol |
| 9 | Sleep, low mood and fear after a heart attack |
| 10 | Family session: inherited cholesterol and checking relatives |
| 11 | Travel, vaccination, devices |

## Risks & things to avoid

- **A mass event for the count.** 5,000-in-a-day drives already exist in Hyderabad. His distinction is a verified skills tick-list and a published retention figure.
- **Screening camps in his or the hospital's name.** Clause 8.1(x) and 8.1(i). Keep Programme A free of blood pressure checks, ECGs and consultation offers.
- **Portrait publicity by hosts.** Associations and companies will make posters with his face. Give them a standard text block and a plain logo-free template in advance; 4.4 keeps him responsible for what he "knowingly permitted".
- **Hospital marketing adopting the programme.** If either hospital brands it around him, 8.3(ii) and 9.2 are engaged. Agree wording first.
- **Over-promising legal immunity** to trainees. s.134A is about road accidents.
- **Donating AEDs without custody.** Year 2 only, with a named custodian, expiry log and quarterly check.
- **WhatsApp advice.** Programme B's group is doctor-to-doctor and de-identified; Programme C's list is broadcast-only.
- **Becoming the referral funnel.** A hospital-neutral transfer list in Programme B is what makes the teaching trustworthy.
- **Patient stories.** Even willing patients, even with consent, cannot be used publicly (6.2).
- **Volunteer fatigue.** A pool of 8–10 instructors with a second cohort in month 9; no one teaches more than twice a month.
- **Competing-hospital politics at SCSC.** Enter as a volunteer with a gap-filling module, not as a hospital.
- **Quoting unverified items from this file:** IRCF fees and pathway, AHA instructor steps, BNS section numbers, the FSSAI trans-fat date. Each is flagged above.
