# Reputation-building for an introverted clinician: the psychology and the operating model

**Research date:** 12 November 2026. **Researcher note on method:** the shared web-search budget ran out part-way through this round, so some items were verified by fetching known primary URLs and a few are stated from background knowledge. Every such item is marked "not re-verified this session". Confidence tags: High / Medium / Low.

## Executive summary

1. The evidence supports a quiet route to reputation: "my doctor" is still the most trusted health voice (80% in 2026, 82% in 2025), third-party praise works better than self-praise, and humble-bragging backfires. An introvert who teaches plainly has the advantage.
2. The NMC's new *Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication* (6 October 2026, in force immediately) make "teach, don't sell" the only safe lane. They bar requesting or sharing patient testimonials or reviews for promotion (consent does not cure it), self-promotion, superiority words, and hold the doctor responsible for anything a third party posts with his approval.
3. The operating model that fits him is interview-extraction: about 60–90 minutes a week of his time (one 20-minute question session, voice notes, one approval pass), with the friend acting as editor and chief of staff. Every clinical claim stays in his words and under his sign-off.
4. Habits take roughly 2–5 months to stick, not 21 days. The plan needs a content buffer before launch, a "never miss twice" rule, a 12-week no-metrics on-ramp, and input-based scorecards.
5. The physician ladder (local, regional, national, international opinion leader) is climbed with publications, trial roles, society positions and invited talks. Social content supports that ladder but does not replace it.

## Findings

### 1. Evidence and expert frameworks on introversion and visibility

**1.1 Doctors start with a trust advantage, but it is being contested**

- In the 2026 Edelman Trust and Health survey (16 countries, about 16,000 respondents, fieldwork 28 Feb–11 Mar 2026), 80% said their doctor is a trusted source of health information and 73% said the same of medical scientists. Confidence in making health decisions fell from 61% (2025) to 51% (2026). India and Mexico tied for second (73%) on "my country is divided over health issues". Source: CIDRAP summary of the report. **Confidence: High** for the global figures; I could not open Edelman's own PDF (403), so numbers come from CIDRAP and Fortune.
- Fortune's coverage of the same report: seven in ten people believe at least one of six debunked health claims; a degree makes no difference (69% vs 70%); 35% use AI to manage their health; trust in media health coverage is 46%. Fortune also reports that personal physicians are the most trusted voice in all 16 markets. Edelman's Jennifer Hauser: "People don't want their doctor to be their guru. They want their doctor to be their guide." Richard Edelman: scientists need to explain the why and how, not only the what. **Confidence: High.**
- A search snippet of the Fortune piece put "divisive health beliefs" highest in India (89%) and South Africa (88%). The fetched article text did not show the India number. **Confidence: Low; could not verify.**
- Yale School of Public Health and Edelman analysis (13 countries including India, about 13,000 people, published Sept 2026): when a doctor and another source conflict, only 51% say they always follow the doctor. Nearly one-third say an uncredentialed person has a major influence on their health decisions. Uncredentialed voices win on personal experience, empathy and easily understood language. **Confidence: High.**
- 2025 edition (reported by TechTarget): "my doctor" 82%; friends and family 72%; 67% find someone with similar lived experience more trustworthy; among 18–34s, 45% preferred friends' and family's advice over their provider and 38% preferred social media; 58% of young adults regretted a health decision based on misinformation. **Confidence: Medium** (secondary source; Edelman page returned 403).
- **India-specific "my doctor" percentage: could not verify.** The APAC country PDF returned 403.

**Implication:** the public still wants its own doctor to speak, but in plain language, with reasons and with empathy. That is a writing and teaching skill, not a performance skill.

**1.2 Online reputation and patient choice**

- rater8 survey of more than 1,000 US adults (reported by Medical Economics, 21 Aug 2025): 84% check online reviews before booking, more than half read at least six; 35% have chosen a physician based on social media presence; 26% said AI recommendations directly influenced their choice; 70% are open to or already use AI tools to research physicians. **Confidence: Medium for the US; Low for India.** The source is a reputation-software vendor with a commercial interest. No peer-reviewed or India-specific study was found in this round.
- Practical reading: his strong Google reviews already do the "patient choice" job. What is missing is the layer that peers, journalists, conference committees and AI answer engines read.

**1.3 Thought leadership evidence (B2B analogy)**

- 2025 Edelman–LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report: 1,934 executives surveyed; focus on "hidden buyers" who influence a decision without being in the room. Secondary coverage reports that hidden buyers consume as much thought leadership as target buyers, and that 45% of decision-makers (48% of C-suite) said thought leadership directly led them to award business. **Confidence: Medium** (Edelman's page returned 403; figures are from secondary coverage and may mix 2024 and 2025 editions).
- Analogy, my inference and not in the report: a cardiologist's "hidden buyers" are referring physicians, programme committees, journalists, hospital leadership and medical-society office bearers. They rarely see him operate; they see what he writes and teaches.

**1.4 Introversion research**

- Susan Cain, *Quiet* (2012): one-third to one-half of people may be introverts. She uses Brian Little's Free Trait Theory: introverts can act out of character for "core personal projects" (work they value, people they love) provided they build in "restorative niches" to recover. Acting against one's nature all the time is unhealthy. **Confidence: High** (book summary; 2012, foundational rather than stale).
- Grant, Gino and Hofmann (Academy of Management Journal, 2011): in 57 pizza stores (374 employees) and a lab study (163 students), introverted leaders got better results when team members were proactive, because they listen and are less concerned with ego. **Confidence: High** on direction; the often-quoted "16% higher profits" figure could not be verified this session, so do not use it.
- Adam Grant, *Give and Take*: givers are found at both the top and the bottom of success rankings; the ones at the bottom are those who get exploited and burn out. **Confidence: High.** Lesson: generosity needs boundaries.
- Cal Newport, *So Good They Can't Ignore You* (2012), subtitle "Why Skills Trump Passion". The book's argument is that rare and valuable skill ("career capital") is the basis for everything else. **Confidence: High** on the thesis; the detailed framework is from background knowledge, not re-verified this session. For him, clinical skill is already the asset. Visibility only reveals it.

**1.5 Why self-praise fails and third-party praise works**

- Pfeffer, Fong, Cialdini and Portnoy (Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2006), three experiments: a person is rated more likeable and competent, and is offered more help, when an intermediary states their strengths. The effect held even when observers knew the intermediary was paid or visibly aligned with the person. **Confidence: High** (2006; classic experiment).
- Cialdini's authority principle: an estate agency whose reception staff mentioned colleagues' credentials before transferring calls saw a 20% rise in appointments and a 15% increase in signed contracts. Physiotherapists who display their diplomas get better exercise compliance. **Confidence: Medium** (Cialdini's own site; original study data not seen).
- Cialdini's social proof: people look to "many similar others" when uncertain. **Confidence: High.**
- Sezer, Gino and Norton (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2018): humblebragging is liked less than plain bragging, and even less than complaining, because it reads as insincere. **Confidence: High.**
- Scopelliti, Loewenstein and Vosgerau (Psychological Science, 2015): self-promoters overestimate how positively others react. **Confidence: Medium; not re-verified this session.**

**1.6 Content frameworks**

- Austin Kleon, *Show Your Work!* (2014). The ten chapters, verified on his site: You don't have to be a genius; Think process, not product; Share something small every day; Open up your cabinet of curiosities; Tell good stories; Teach what you know; Don't turn into human spam; Learn to take a punch; Sell out; Stick around. The book is written for people who dislike self-promotion. **Confidence: High.**
- Dorie Clark's "recognised expert" formula: content creation, social proof, network. All three are needed. She advises picking one channel, producing concentrated content on one topic for about 18 months, not giving up at 3 or 6 months, and giving before asking. She did 160 podcast interviews per book launch. **Confidence: High** (Thinkific interview summary).
- Jonah Berger, *Contagious* (2013): STEPPS stands for Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories. **Confidence: High** on the six names (background knowledge; Berger's site lists the STEPPS framework but gates the detail). For a doctor, "practical value" and "stories" are the two that can be used without hype.
- "Document, don't create" (Gary Vaynerchuk): share what you are already doing instead of inventing content. **Confidence: Medium; original URL returned 404, not re-verified.**

**1.7 Burnout versus meaning**

- Shanafelt et al., Archives of Internal Medicine 2009 (465 academic physicians): 34% met burnout criteria. Those who spent less than 20% of their time on the activity they found most meaningful had a burnout rate of 53.8% versus 29.9%. Patient care was most meaningful for 68%, research for 19%, education for 9%. **Confidence: High** (2009, US academic setting; possibly stale for India).
- Reading: the programme must be built around what he personally finds meaningful (ask him: teaching juniors, patient education, or research), and it must not eat into that time. No evidence was found that public teaching itself reduces burnout; that is a hypothesis.

**1.8 Harassment risk**

- Pendergrast et al., JAMA Internal Medicine 2021 (464 US physicians, surveyed 2019): 23.3% reported being personally attacked on social media; 16.4% of women reported sexual harassment. Attacks included review-bombing and coordinated harassment. Authors recommend institutional response plans and advocating as a group. **Confidence: High** for the US; no Indian figure found.

### 2. The regulatory frame that shapes every reframe (India)

I read the 13-page NMC public notice directly (No. R-13014/01/2024-Ethics, dated 06/10/2026, digitally signed by the NMC Secretary; copy hosted by Medical Dialogues). I could not locate it on nmc.org.in (guessed URL returned 404), but four outlets report it. **Confidence: High that it exists and on the quoted text; Medium on interpretation. A lawyer or the State Medical Council should confirm the readings below.**

Clauses that matter most for this project:

| Clause | What it says (close paraphrase or quote) | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 3.2 | "Advertisement" is any communication that solicits, promotes or "is intended to promote the professional services, reputation, skills, qualifications, achievements" of a doctor. Digital, influencer-led and AI-assisted content counts when it has "a promotional character". | The project's intent and vocabulary matter. "PR" and "brand" are risky words; "public education and scholarship" is the accurate frame. |
| 3.2 Expl. I | Information given to the public in the normal course, non-promotional and without commercial intent, is exempt. | This is the lane. |
| 3.2 Expl. III | A doctor publishing electronic media posts "should transparently disclose their name, qualifications, registration status and SMR/NMR Registration number". | Registration number goes in every bio and on long-form pieces. Whether it must be on each post is unclear. |
| 3.2 Expl. V | "An RMP shall not request or share patient testimonials, recommendations, endorsements or reviews for professional promotion on social media." | No asking for reviews; no reposting Google reviews. |
| 4.4 | Publishing through a third party or agency does not absolve the doctor for content he "authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted". | He is answerable for everything the friend or an AI tool posts. |
| 5.1 | Doctors may take part in health awareness, public health campaigns, academic discussion and educational content, if it does not promote personal practice, solicit patients or get monetised through promotional marketing. | Explicit permission for teaching. |
| 6.2 | Patient consent does not by itself make a testimonial, patient story or success claim permissible. | "Patient voices with consent" is not a safe reframe for promotion. |
| 7.2 | AI-generated promotional campaigns are prohibited; AI must not create synthetic patient or doctor endorsements; material AI use must be disclosed; patient data fed to AI must meet privacy law. | No patient data into AI tools. Disclose AI assistance where it matters. |
| 7.4 | No sharing of publicly identifiable patient data (names, faces, anomalies). | Images and case details need strict de-identification. |
| 8.1(ii) | A doctor shall not promote services through self-promotion, or "engage any third party for marketing medical services". | The friend's role must be editorial support for education, not marketing. |
| 8.1(iii), (ix) | No advertising of personal achievements, success rates or number of patients treated with assertions like "Best doctors", "No.1 Specialist"; no "best", "leading", "top", "most trusted" unless objectively verifiable. | Review his current hospital profile numbers and any award language. |
| 8.1(iv) | No endorsement of any drug, device or product using his name, photo, voice or status, paid or unpaid. | Shapes all industry-sponsored speaking. |
| 8.1(v) | No publishing cases for promotion, celebrity patients or success stories, "unless published strictly for scientific or educational purposes with anonymized patient consent". | Case-of-the-week is possible only as peer education, anonymised, with consent. |
| 8.1(xi), (xii) | No using patients, employees or third parties for prohibited testimonials; no fake followers, likes, reviews or search manipulation. | Organic only. |
| 8.2 | Allowed: research-based communication about case studies published in reputed journals; public-health lectures on TV, radio and electronic media "in their own name and designation, without promoting their employer organization". | Publish first, then talk about it. Media talks in own name are permitted. |
| 8.3(ii) | Hospital communication must not include "promotional portrayal of an individual RMP". | The hospital marketing team cannot be the main engine for his personal visibility. |
| 10.1 | Graded penalties: warning and ethics training, then censure and fine, then suspension (3–6 months), up to removal for 1–3 years. | Real downside. |

Net effect: the regulation rewards exactly the behaviour a humble clinician is comfortable with, and penalises the behaviour he dislikes.

### 3. Reframes that make visibility acceptable (checked against evidence and the rules)

| Reframe | Evidence base | Regulatory status | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teaching, not boasting | Kleon ("Teach what you know"); Edelman (explain why and how) | Expressly allowed (5.1, 8.2 iii) | Core reframe |
| Document, don't create | Kleon ("Think process, not product"); Vaynerchuk | Allowed if no patient identifiers | Core workflow |
| Let others praise | Pfeffer 2006; Cialdini | Split. Peer recognition of his teaching and science (invitations, citations, a chair's introduction) is fine. Patient or third-party endorsements of his services are barred (Expl. V, 6.2, 8.1 xi) | Use only the peer and scholarly half |
| Patient voices with consent | Edelman "lived experience" finding | Not safe for promotion; consent does not cure (6.2). Unsolicited Google reviews may simply exist; do not request or repost them | Drop as a tactic |
| Credit the team | Grant (introverted leaders listen, less ego) | No issue; avoid employer promotion in public-health talks | Core habit |
| Service orientation | Free Trait Theory: a "core personal project" licenses out-of-character effort | Matches the "no commercial intent" exemption | Core motive |
| Cost of silence: misinformation fills the gap | Edelman 2026: 7 in 10 hold a debunked belief; 51% always follow the doctor; uncredentialed voices growing | Supports public-interest framing | Strongest moral argument |

A sentence he may accept: "I am not asking anyone to choose me. I am making sure that what people in Telangana hear about heart disease is true."

### 4. Low-social-energy formats and workflows

Energy ratings are practitioner judgment (no study found). Time is his time only.

| Format | His time | Social energy | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interview-extraction: friend asks 5 questions, records 20 min, turns it into 2–3 written pieces | 20 min/week | Low (one trusted person) | The engine. Ghostwriting guides list recorded interviews and voice memos as the standard voice-capture method |
| Voice note after a case or clinic ("what I wish patients knew today") | 2–3 min, 2–3 times/week | Very low | No names, dates, bed numbers or identifying details. Keep recordings out of AI tools if they contain patient data |
| Asynchronous Q&A: friend collects 5 public questions, he answers by voice or text | 15 min/week | Low | Answer the general question, never the individual case |
| Slides-to-posts: every CME or departmental talk becomes 3–5 posts | 0 extra (approval only) | Very low | Best ratio of output to effort |
| Case-of-the-week teaching pearl (for doctors) | 15 min | Low | Anonymised, consent on file, educational framing only (8.1 v). Safest route: publish in a journal first, then discuss (8.2 i) |
| Recorded teaching rounds or journal club (audio or slides only) | 0 extra | Low (he is already teaching) | Hospital permission; no patient audio or images |
| Written column (English and Telugu) | 30 min/fortnight from interview | Low | Permitted as public education in own name and designation |
| Podcast guesting | 45–60 min/month | Medium | Guest, do not host: no production burden, borrowed audience. Clark used guesting at scale |
| 1:1 journalist briefing | 20–30 min when needed | Medium | Prefer over press meets; one reporter, one topic, written key facts sent afterwards. Note that "public interviews, news articles intended for promotion" count as advertising media (3.5); keep it educational |
| Short video to camera | 30 min for 2–3 clips | High | Last on the ladder, optional |

**Camera shyness: a graded ladder** (judgment, consistent with Free Trait Theory): audio only, then voice over slides, then being filmed while teaching residents (looking at them, not the lens), then seated conversation with the friend off-camera, then direct to camera. Move up only when the previous step feels boring. Batch recordings and schedule a restorative block afterwards.

**Energy rules:** one public-facing "out of character" event per week at most; recording on a fixed low-load slot; nothing on post-call days.

**Boundaries (publish these in the bio or a pinned post):**
- No medical advice in comments or DMs. Standard reply: "I can't advise on individual cases online. Please see your doctor; in an emergency call 108 or go to the nearest hospital." (108 as the emergency ambulance number in Telangana is from background knowledge; confirm.)
- DMs are triaged by the friend during set hours; nothing is answered late at night.
- He never argues in comments. One factual reply, then silence.

### 5. The chief-of-staff / "PR friend" model

**What is normal:** in DSMN8's dataset of 1,118 executive LinkedIn posts (Jan 2025–Sep 2026), delegates wrote 56% of executive posts, and executives reviewed and decided what went out. Delegate-written posts reached more people, but posts executives wrote themselves had a higher engagement rate (4.26% vs 2.49%). The data "points toward deliberate over frequent posting". **Confidence: Medium** (vendor dataset, corporate not medical).

**What makes it ethical** (synthesis of ICMJE, PRSA and NMC):
- Ideas and judgments originate with him. The writer shapes, never invents. (Ghostwriting guidance: ethical when the ideas originate with the named person.)
- He gives final approval and is accountable. (ICMJE authorship requires final approval and accountability; NMC 4.4 makes him responsible in any case.)
- For journal articles, writing help must be acknowledged, AI use disclosed, and AI cannot be an author. Humans are responsible for all AI-assisted material. (ICMJE.) Ghost authorship of scientific papers is not acceptable.
- Avoid deception; disclose paid relationships. (PRSA Code: "Avoid deceptive practices", "Reveal the sponsors".) PRSA's page does not address ghostwriting directly; **its specific ghostwriting advisory could not be verified.**
- Suggested disclosure line for his bio (my recommendation, not a quoted norm): "Posts are prepared with editorial help. All medical content is reviewed and approved by me."

**What must remain his own words:** any clinical claim, number, risk statement, drug or device mention, opinion on a guideline or trial, anything about a specific case, any reply to a patient, and any comment on colleagues or institutions.

**Division of roles**

| Task | Dr. Avinash | Friend (chief of staff) | Hospital marketing | AI tools |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Choose topics and point of view | Decides | Proposes from questions seen in public | Shares calendar (heart day, camps) | Suggests question lists |
| Raw material | Speaks for 20 min; voice notes | Interviews, records, files | — | Transcribes non-patient audio |
| Drafting | — | Writes in his voice | — | First drafts, Telugu/English versions, slide-to-post conversion |
| Clinical accuracy | Sole owner | Flags doubts | — | Never the authority |
| Compliance check (NMC list) | Final say | Runs checklist on every piece | Confirms hospital policy | Can pre-screen for banned words |
| Approval | Yes/no/edit in one weekly batch | Chases, logs approvals | Informed, not approver of his personal content | — |
| Publishing and scheduling | — | Does it | Hospital channels only, factual directory content | — |
| Comments and DMs | Replies to peers when he wishes | Triage, standard replies | — | — |
| Media and conference outreach | Attends | Prepares briefs, follows up | Coordinates hospital press policy | Drafts briefing notes |
| Metrics and quarterly review | Reviews once a quarter | Maintains the ledger | — | Summarises |

Three cautions specific to the friend: no payment or benefit linked to patients or referrals (8.1 vii, viii); the friend's own large LinkedIn/X following should amplify his teaching, not recommend his services (8.1 xi); and the friend must never post unapproved text under his name.

### 6. Personal brand foundations (internal working documents)

- **Name:** pick one public spelling and use it everywhere. Anchor it to the name on his medical council registration, because the registration number must now be disclosed with posts. Use "also known as" on the website for the variant ("Dr. Pathuri Avinash" / "Dr. Avinash Paturi"). Create an ORCID record under the chosen form so publications attach to one identity (ORCID suggestion is practitioner judgment).
- **Positioning statement (internal only; draft to test with him, not a fact about him):** "An interventional cardiologist in Hyderabad who explains heart disease and its treatment honestly, in Telugu and English, so that patients decide well and young doctors learn faster." No superlatives; nothing that could be read as "best" or "leading".
- **Content pillars (pick 3–5; drafts):** (1) Heart attack: recognise it, act in time. (2) Honest answers about stents, angiography and pacemakers, including when not to do a procedure. (3) Prevention for Telugu families: blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, tobacco. (4) Teaching pearls for residents and physicians. (5) Reading the evidence: what a new trial or guideline means in practice.
- **Point of view:** one or two things he believes that are not generic. To be extracted in the first long interview (for example his view on over-testing, or on delays before patients reach a cath lab). Do not invent this for him.
- **Origin story:** why cardiology, a teacher who shaped him, a case type that changed how he practises (no identifiable patient). 150 words, told once, reused.
- **Proof assets that are permitted:** qualifications and registration; publications and conference presentations; teaching roles; society memberships; invited talks; a body of educational content. **Proof assets to avoid in promotion:** procedure counts as selling points, success rates, reposted reviews, patient stories, awards without verifiable methodology.
- **Tone of voice:** calm, specific, plain words, reasons given, uncertainty admitted, no fear appeals (8.1 i bars fear-based marketing), no criticism of other doctors. "Guide, not guru."
- **Bilingual voice:** IAMAI–Kantar *Internet in India 2024* reports about 886 million active users, 98% of whom accessed content in Indic languages, and 57% of urban users prefer Indic-language content; press coverage names Telugu among the most-used languages. **Confidence: Medium** (press reports; no Telugu-only figure). Working rule (judgment): English for peers and journals; spoken-style Telugu with familiar English medical terms for the public; every Telugu piece read by him or a Telugu-fluent editor before publishing, never machine translation alone.

### 7. Habit design

**Evidence:** Singh et al. 2024 systematic review (20 studies): median time to habit formation 59–66 days, means 106–154 days, individual range 4–335 days; health habits typically need 2–5 months. Self-chosen habits and morning practice were stronger; planning and enjoyment helped. **Confidence: High.** James Clear: make it "easy enough that you can get it done without motivation"; "never miss twice"; "consistent, not perfect". Dorie Clark: do not judge at 3 or 6 months.

**Minimum viable cadence (floor, not target):** one 20-minute session a week and one published piece a week. On a bad week the floor drops to one 2-minute voice note. The streak counted is "weeks with a session", not posts or likes.

**12-week on-ramp from zero**

| Weeks | What happens | His time |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (setup) | Name decision; registration details; red-lines sheet; hospital conversation; 90-minute voice-capture interview; pillars agreed | 3–4 hours once |
| 1–2 | Private only. Two interview sessions a week. Build a bank of 10–12 finished pieces. Nothing published | 40 min/week |
| 3–6 | One English written post a week plus its Telugu version. No video. Friend handles all replies | 45–60 min/week |
| 7–9 | Add a fortnightly teaching pearl for doctors, and turn one existing talk into posts. First podcast guest slot or one journalist briefing | 60–75 min/week |
| 10–12 | First low-stakes recorded teaching (slides and voice). Submit one case report or abstract. Week-12 review: keep, cut, or change | 60–90 min/week |

**How not to quit at week 6** (design choices, judgment informed by the evidence above):
- A four-piece buffer always in hand, so a bad fortnight does not break the public rhythm.
- Three operating modes agreed in advance: green (full), amber (interview only), red (one voice note). Scope shrinks; the habit does not stop.
- No audience numbers shown to him before week 12. The friend watches them.
- Fixed slot tied to an existing routine (for example after a set weekly list), morning if possible.
- The friend holds the appointment like a clinic slot and asks the first question; he never faces a blank page.
- Reduce, don't add: one channel for English, one for Telugu, until week 12.

**Measuring progress without vanity metrics**
- Inputs: sessions held, pieces approved, weeks in streak.
- Signals of trust: messages from doctors, questions from residents, patients mentioning something they read, saves and shares by clinicians.
- Ladder markers: invitations to teach or speak, abstracts and papers submitted and accepted, society committee roles, journalist requests, citations.
- A quarterly one-page "reputation ledger" listing these. Follower counts are recorded but not targeted. Buying or manipulating engagement is prohibited (8.1 xii).

**Trolls, criticism, peer jealousy and hospital politics**
- Expect it: about one in four physicians on social media reported personal attacks (US, 2019 data).
- 24-hour rule for any criticism; the friend reads first; one factual correction at most; block and document abuse. Kleon: "Learn to take a punch."
- Correct claims, never people. No comments on other doctors' cases, fees or hospitals.
- Peer jealousy: credit seniors and the team by name where they agree; invite colleagues to co-teach; share the platform with juniors, nurses and cath-lab staff; cite others' work generously (Clark: give before asking).
- Hospital politics: tell the department head and hospital communications before launch; get the hospital's social media policy in writing; keep personal channels free of hospital branding in public-health content (8.2 iii) and free of any criticism of the institution.
- Agree in advance who he calls if a post goes wrong (friend first, then hospital communications, then a medico-legal adviser).

### 8. Physician thought-leadership ladders (opinion-leader tiers)

Most published tier models come from vendors serving pharma and med-tech medical affairs teams; there is no single industry standard, and none of the sources found is India-specific. **Confidence: Medium on the pattern, Low on exact thresholds.**

| Tier | Typical profile | Illustrative criteria (IntuitionLabs example table; "varies by company") | Typical engagement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local / community | Skilled clinician known in a city or hospital; peers call him for difficult cases; trains juniors | Fewer than 5 publications or mainly clinical; high local volume; hospital committees; small engaged network | Departmental teaching, grand rounds, local society chapter meetings |
| Regional | Recognised across a state or metro area | About 5–20 publications; multicentre or regional protocol participation; leadership in local chapters or regional meetings; referral expert; moderate digital presence | Regional CME faculty, regional advisory panels |
| National | Major institutional affiliation; national conference faculty; trial investigator | About 30+ publications at the top end; national guideline or society board roles; principal investigator on major trials; awards | National congress faculty, advisory boards, guideline writing |
| International | Influence across countries | Cross-border research collaboration, international congress faculty, high citation counts | Global steering committees, international faculty |

**What moves a doctor up** (consistent across sources): peer-reviewed publications and citations; trial investigator roles; elected or committee positions in specialty societies; invitations to speak or chair (a sign of peer selection); being the person others refer difficult cases to; and, increasingly, a credible digital teaching presence ("digital opinion leaders"). Geography or follower counts alone do not do it. Tiers are usually reviewed yearly.

**India cautions:** (a) NMC 8.1(iv) bars endorsing any drug or device with his name, photo or voice, paid or not, so industry-sponsored talks must stay educational and non-product. (b) India's Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices 2024 and its medical-device counterpart restrict gifts, travel and hospitality and set conditions for engaging doctors as speakers. **Not re-verified this session; another round should read the primary text.** (c) Relevant society names and meetings in interventional cardiology (for example the Cardiological Society of India and its Telangana chapter) were not researched here.

## So what for Dr. Avinash

Prioritised. Time is his own unless stated.

1. **Adopt the frame "public education and scholarship", not "PR".** Write a one-page charter: purpose is accurate heart-health information and teaching; no solicitation; no superlatives. One-off, 30 minutes. This protects him under clause 3.2 and matches his temperament.
2. **Run a compliance reset before anything is published.** Friend builds a checklist from the NMC clauses above; get a medico-legal opinion on the open points (registration number on each post, case teaching, existing profile numbers, responding to reviews). One-off, 1 hour of his time plus the adviser's fee.
3. **Stop anything that asks for or reposts reviews and testimonials.** Leave existing unsolicited Google reviews alone. One-off, 10 minutes to instruct everyone.
4. **Fix the name.** Choose one spelling matched to his registration; add registration number and qualifications to every bio. One-off, 1 hour (friend does most of it).
5. **Start the weekly 20-minute interview.** Friend asks five real questions from patients or junior doctors. This is the whole engine. 20 minutes/week.
6. **Add voice notes after clinic.** Two or three a week, 2 minutes each, no patient identifiers.
7. **One approval batch a week.** He reads, corrects and approves everything in a single sitting. 15–20 minutes/week. Nothing goes out without it.
8. **Build a 10–12 piece bank before launch (weeks 1–2), then publish one English and one Telugu written piece a week.** No video for the first six weeks.
9. **Turn every talk he already gives into posts.** Zero extra time; the friend converts slides.
10. **Put one scholarly item in motion each quarter** (case report, abstract, review, or audit). This is what moves him up the opinion-leader ladder, and published work may then be discussed publicly under 8.2(i). 1–2 hours/month.
11. **Seek borrowed platforms once a month:** a podcast guest slot, a Telugu newspaper column, a CME faculty slot, or a 1:1 journalist briefing. 30–60 minutes/month.
12. **Set boundaries publicly:** no advice in comments, no late DMs, friend triages. One-off, 15 minutes.
13. **Tell the hospital first.** A short conversation with the department head and hospital communications; get their policy in writing. One-off, 30 minutes.
14. **Review at week 12 and every quarter** using the reputation ledger, not follower counts. 30 minutes/quarter.

Steady state: about 60–90 minutes a week for him; about 3–4 hours a week for the friend (my estimate).

## Risks & things to avoid

- **Regulatory:** treating the programme as marketing; patient testimonials or stories even with consent; reposting reviews; "best / leading / top / most trusted"; procedure counts and success rates as selling points; before-and-after or identifiable images; fear-based messaging; AI-generated promotional campaigns; undisclosed AI where it matters; patient data in AI tools; product endorsements; paid or manipulated engagement; hospital channels hyping him as an individual.
- **Authenticity:** the friend's corporate LinkedIn style leaking into a doctor's voice; posts a generic AI could have written; humble-bragging; unapproved posts under his name.
- **Clinical:** individual advice online; statements outside interventional cardiology; commenting on news cases without facts.
- **Personal:** over-scheduling public events without recovery time; letting the programme eat into patient care or family time; reading comments at night.
- **Political:** surprising hospital leadership; appearing to compete with seniors; criticising colleagues or institutions; taking sole credit for team outcomes.
- **Evidence limits:** most patient-choice and thought-leadership statistics here are US or corporate data from vendors. They justify direction, not targets.
